The **Honda CR-V** has been on sale in the Philippines since **1997** — longer than any other Honda SUV, longer than the [City](/blog/honda-city-pms-guide), and long enough that the ones you see on the road every day span nearly thirty years and six distinct vehicles. That is unusual, and it changes how you have to read a maintenance guide for it. A CR-V is not one car with one service bill. It is six.

This guide covers the **2026 lineup** properly — costs, intervals, the recall status, and the warranty fine print — and then does something the newer PMS guides on this site have not had to do: it works backwards through **every generation Honda Cars Philippines actually sold here**, because a large share of CR-Vs on Philippine roads are twenty-year-old ones, and their maintenance realities are nothing like the new car's.

The headline for new buyers is a decision the brochure buries. **The 2026 CR-V's only turbo variant is also its only seven-seater** — and it is the cheapest one. When the VX switched to the hybrid powertrain this year, it dropped to five seats. So if you need a third row, the configurator makes the choice for you. This is the CR-V instalment of our [full explainer on what PMS means, when it's due and what it costs](/blog/car-pms-philippines-guide).

## Key Takeaways

- **PMS is every 10,000 km or 6 months** — Honda Cars Philippines' brand-wide cadence, now verified across the City, Brio, Jazz, BR-V and HR-V. Against the 5,000 km diesel-SUV competition, that is **two casa visits a year instead of four.**
- **Only the base 1.5 V Turbo has seven seats in 2026.** The VX went hybrid and lost the third row. Need a third row, and the cheapest variant is your only CR-V.
- **The V Turbo has a real CVT and needs Honda HCF-2 fluid. The e:HEV's "E-CVT" is not a CVT** — no belt, no pulleys, no CVT-fluid worry.
- **Every CR-V engine sold here is timing-chain driven except one:** the first-generation **B20B (1997–2001) runs a timing belt.** That is the single most important question to ask about an RD1.
- **AWD units have a rear differential that needs Honda Dual Pump Fluid on a short interval** — a genuinely neglected item, and an expensive one when it's been ignored.
- **The five-year warranty is real but conditional.** Honda Secure Plus gives **5 years / 150,000 km** on 2026-model-year CR-Vs at no extra cost — **only if you follow the PMS schedule at an authorised dealer.** 2023–2025 units stay at 3 years / 100,000 km.
- **The CR-V is named in a PH recall:** 1,351 units of the **2023–2024 RS e:HEV** for a high-pressure fuel pump. Run your VIN.
- **5 stars at ASEAN NCAP, 87.16 overall**, with a near-perfect 39.31/40 for adult occupant protection.
- **Honda's PM Cost Calculator is still offline** — checked again on **7 September 2026.** No PH manufacturer publishes per-model PMS pricing, and Honda's one attempt at it remains down.

Three notes on that table, because the numbers move around depending on where you read them.

**On the turbo's output:** you will see **190 PS** on some pages and **187 hp** on others. These are the same engine. 190 PS converts to roughly 187 hp — it is a unit difference, not a variant difference. Torque is **240 Nm**, available from **1,700 to 5,000 rpm**, which is the number that actually matters in traffic.

**On the hybrid's output:** the 2.0-litre Atkinson-cycle engine makes **146 hp and 183 Nm**, and the electric side is quoted at **181 hp and 335 Nm** combined. **We are not printing a single "system output" figure**, because the sources we could verify quote the engine and the motors separately, and a combined number would be us doing arithmetic Honda has not published. The relevant point for a driver: in an e:HEV, the motors do most of the driving, and the torque figure that describes how it feels off the line is the electric one.

**On "HuNT":** it is in the variant's official name — *1.5 V Turbo **HuNT** CVT* — and we could not find any Honda Cars Philippines material that explains what it stands for. We are flagging that rather than guessing at an expansion. If a salesperson tells you it means something specific, ask them to show you where Honda says so.

## The Service Schedule: Every 10,000 km or 6 Months

Honda Cars Philippines runs a **10,000 km / 6-month** PMS cadence across the range. Honda's own materials describe this as longer than what most other manufacturers here require, and that is simply true.

On a CR-V it lands harder than on a City, because the CR-V's natural rivals are diesel seven-seat SUVs sitting on a **5,000 km** light-PMS cycle:

| Annual distance | CR-V visits/yr | [Fortuner](/blog/toyota-fortuner-pms-guide) / [Montero Sport](/blog/mitsubishi-montero-sport-pms-guide) / [mu-X](/blog/isuzu-mu-x-pms-guide) visits/yr |
|---|---|---|
| 10,000 km | 1 | 2 |
| 20,000 km | 2 | 4 |
| To 100,000 km | **10 oil changes** | **20 oil changes** |

<Pullquote>Half the service visits over the life of the car. On a vehicle you will keep for a decade, that is not a rounding error — it is a different ownership experience.</Pullquote>

Broadly: the light service is oil and filter plus an inspection round. The heavier work — air cleaner element, cabin filter, brake fluid, spark plugs, and the transmission and differential fluids — clusters at the **40,000 km and 80,000 km** stops.

**What we will not print: the oil grade and capacity.** Honda PH publishes no per-model figure for the CR-V, and the numbers circulating online come from generic engine databases that do not distinguish between the 1.5 turbo and the 2.0 Atkinson. We have hit this same wall on the [BR-V](/blog/honda-br-v-pms-guide), the [HR-V](/blog/honda-hr-v-pms-guide), the [Suzuki S-Presso](/blog/suzuki-s-presso-pms-guide) and the [Mitsubishi Xforce](/blog/mitsubishi-xforce-pms-guide). **Your owner's manual is the authority.** A blank row beats a wrong viscosity, and on a turbocharged engine a wrong viscosity is not a small mistake.

## The Seven-Seat Trap in the 2026 Lineup

This is the section that will change someone's purchase.

Since the sixth generation arrived in **September 2023**, the turbo variants have been the seven-seaters and the hybrid has been a five-seater. In the 2023–2025 lineup that gave you two seven-seat options: the **V Turbo** and the **VX Turbo CVT AWD.**

**For 2026, the VX switched to the e:HEV hybrid — and became a five-seater.** The RS e:HEV took over AWD duty with a real-time system. Which leaves the lineup like this:

- Want **seven seats**? Only the **1.5 V Turbo**, the cheapest variant. No hybrid, no AWD.
- Want the **hybrid**? Five seats, and you are choosing between FWD (VX) and AWD (RS).
- Want **AWD**? Only the **RS e:HEV**, the most expensive variant, and it seats five.

There is no overlap. You cannot buy a seven-seat hybrid CR-V, and you cannot buy a seven-seat AWD CR-V. **Every combination you might actually want forces you to give one of the three up.**

On the third row itself, contemporary local reviews of the gen-6 turbo are consistent and fair: adults up to about **170 cm** fit, access is a single-lever fold on the second row, and the honest verdict is that it is **excellent as a five-seater and acceptable as a seven.** Cargo behind a raised third row is **150 litres** — enough for a grocery run, not a provincial trip. Drop it and you get **581 litres**; fold the second row too and it is **1,064 litres.**

If seven genuinely-usable seats are non-negotiable, the honest answer is that this segment has better answers — our [comparison of the Fortuner, Montero Sport, mu-X and Everest](/blog/fortuner-vs-montero-vs-mux-vs-everest) covers the body-on-frame side, and the [Innova Zenix](/blog/toyota-innova-zenix-pms-guide) is the hybrid seven-seater the CR-V does not offer.

## Transmissions: Three Very Different Things

Across the CR-V range, past and present, you are dealing with three fundamentally different gearboxes, and they ask for completely different care.

**The CVT (V Turbo, and the gen-5 2.0 petrol).** A conventional belt-and-pulley CVT, same principle as the City and Jazz. It has one non-negotiable requirement: **Honda HCF-2 fluid.** Honda specifically excludes damage caused by non-equivalent fluid from warranty coverage. A shop that shrugs and pours in generic ATF because *"CVT fluid din naman"* is setting up a failure that costs more than every PMS you have ever paid for.

The same gap we found on the Jazz, BR-V and HR-V applies here: **no Honda PH document we could locate states a CVT-fluid replacement interval in kilometres for the CR-V.** The approach used across this site is to treat the **40,000 km heavy service** as the point to do it, and to say openly that we are applying a convention rather than quoting a spec. **Get your dealer to write the interval into your service booklet.**

**The E-CVT (VX and RS e:HEV).** Not a CVT. Honda's e:HEV is an **i-MMD**-type twin-motor system — a traction motor, a generator motor, and an engine that spends most of its life generating rather than driving, through a **fixed-gear transaxle with a lock-up clutch** for high-speed cruising. There is **no belt and there are no pulleys**, so there is no CVT-fluid anxiety at all. What you get instead:

- **Brakes that last considerably longer**, because regenerative braking does much of the slowing. Worth knowing before a shop tells you it is pad time.
- **New inspection items**: the hybrid battery, its cooling, and the high-voltage system. Casa work, not talyer work.
- **Oil that still ages on the calendar** even though the engine runs less of the time. Do not let a low odometer talk you past the six-month leg of the interval.

**The 9-speed automatic (gen-5 1.6 diesel only).** If you are shopping used, this is the one to go in with your eyes open. It is a torque-converter nine-speed, not a CVT, and it is the only CR-V gearbox here with that many ratios. On a second-hand diesel, a **transmission service history and a long test drive covering low-speed shifts** are worth more than any spec sheet. Ask when the fluid was last changed and by whom.

## Timing Belt or Chain?

**Chain — on everything except the first generation.**

- **1.5 VTEC Turbo** (2023–): chain
- **2.0 e:HEV Atkinson** (2023–): chain
- **1.6 i-DTEC diesel** (2017–2022): chain
- **2.0 petrol / R20A** (2007–2022): chain
- **K20A and K24** (2002–2016): chain — the entire K-series family is chain-driven
- **B20B** (1997–2001): **timing BELT**

That last line is the whole reason this section exists. If you are looking at a first-generation RD1, the timing belt is a **scheduled replacement job, not a lifetime part**, and it should be done together with the water pump. Honda's own overseas guidance for the B20B is around **seven years or 105,000 miles**, but on a car that is now more than twenty-five years old in Philippine heat and humidity, **age governs, not mileage.** A 90,000 km RD1 with its original belt is not a low-mileage find; it is an overdue job.

For every other CR-V, the chain asks for one thing: **clean oil, on schedule.** That is the entire maintenance plan for it.

## The AWD Item Nobody Services

If you are buying or already own **any all-wheel-drive CR-V** — the RS e:HEV, the 2023–25 VX Turbo AWD, the gen-5 SX Diesel AWD, or any of the older Real Time 4WD cars — there is a maintenance item that gets skipped constantly.

Honda's Real Time AWD system uses a rear differential with **internal clutch packs**, and those clutches require **Honda Dual Pump Fluid** (DPSF / Dual Pump Fluid II). This is not a place for generic gear oil or ATF. The wrong fluid produces noise, poor engagement, and eventually damages the clutch assembly.

It also needs changing more often than people expect. The clutch packs generate heat, the fluid is low-viscosity by design, and it shears down and picks up clutch material far faster than the gear oil in a conventional transfer case. **Honda's own overseas schedule puts the first change at around 15,000 miles (roughly 24,000 km) and every 30,000 miles (roughly 48,000 km) after that** — and that interval was reportedly shortened precisely because differentials were getting noisy before the original, longer interval came due.

**HCPI publishes no local figure for it**, which is exactly why it gets missed here. Two practical consequences:

1. **If you own an AWD CR-V**, ask your dealer to confirm the rear differential fluid interval in writing and to note it in your booklet. It is a cheap service.
2. **If you are buying a used AWD CR-V**, ask when it was last done. The classic symptom of a neglected one is **noise or binding on tight, slow turns** — a car park manoeuvre will often reveal it. Budget for a fluid change on any unit whose owner cannot answer the question.

## Known Issues, By Generation

Honest scope note first: **we found no documented widespread mechanical failure pattern for the Philippine sixth-generation CR-V.** It is a 2023 car here. We are reporting that as "we did not find one," not as "there is none." What follows is what is actually documented, tagged by which car it applies to.

**Sixth generation (2023–), RS e:HEV — high-pressure fuel pump.** A live PH recall, covered in full below. Real, named, and free to fix.

**Fifth generation (2017–2022), 1.6 i-DTEC diesel — DPF and injectors.** The 1.6 i-DTEC has a well-documented reputation for **diesel particulate filter clogging when used mainly for short urban trips**, where the filter never gets hot enough to regenerate. The failure mode is a warning light and, in the worse cases, limp mode. Injector failures on the same engine are also documented.

**Two important caveats on that**, because they change how much weight you should give it. First, **the substantial body of evidence for both issues is European** — UK owners' forums, EU independent specialists, and repair costs quoted in pounds and euros. That is a different climate, a different duty cycle, and different fuel. Second, we found **no Philippine recall or HCPI technical bulletin** for either. So: treat it as a real risk factor to inspect for, especially on a city-only car, not as a confirmed Philippine defect rate. If you are looking at a used 1.6 diesel, ask directly whether the DPF has ever been cleaned or replaced, and be honest with yourself about your own driving pattern. **A diesel that only ever does Alabang-to-Makati in traffic is the exact use case this engine dislikes.**

**Second generation (2002–2006) — the third row.** Not a defect, but worth naming: the "10-seater" configuration existed for tax classification. Do not buy one expecting a seven-seat family car.

**First generation (1997–2001) — age, not design.** Timing belt, rust in the usual places on a car this old, and a Real Time 4WD rear differential that has statistically almost certainly gone too long on its fluid. None of this is a fault in the car. It is a twenty-five-year-old vehicle, and it should be priced and inspected like one.

## Recalls: What HCPI Actually Published

Honda Cars Philippines maintains a recall page with a **Chassis/VIN search tool**, and that is the authoritative place to check your specific unit. Here is what the current campaigns cover.

**High-pressure fuel pump — the CR-V is named.** HCPI's own detail page covers **select CR-V RS e:HEV E-CVT units, model years 2023 to 2024**, totalling **1,351 units.** The pumps may have **cracks on the Core B** component, which can allow petrol fumes to escape — the symptom an owner notices is **smelling fuel while idling or driving.** Inspection, parts and labour are **free of charge.**

**Steering gearbox — 5,505 units, no model list.** HCPI announced a second recall for a steering gearbox fault: grease in the **worm gear meshing area** decreases with use, sliding resistance and torque fluctuation rise, and the result is **unusual noise and "sticky steering"** while turning. Parts and labour free. **HCPI did not publish which models are affected** — its announcement describes the defect without naming vehicles. We cannot tell you whether the CR-V is in it, and neither can anyone else who is being straight with you.

Servicing for both began **17 January 2025**, at Honda's **38 dealerships and three service centres** nationwide.

**What to do about it takes one minute:** enter your **17-digit chassis number or VIN** into the search tool at `hondaphil.com/recall`. Free, definitive for your specific car, and it settles the steering question without anyone having to speculate.

## The American CR-V Problem

Search "Honda CR-V 1.5 turbo problems" and you will find a great deal of material about **oil dilution** — fuel passing the piston rings during cold running, diluting the engine oil, producing a fuel smell at the dipstick and a **rising** oil level. It is a real, thoroughly documented issue. Honda issued **TSB 19-039**, extended the powertrain warranty on affected cars, pushed ECU updates that changed cold-start fuelling, and settled a US class action over it.

Now the two facts that determine whether any of that applies to you.

**First: it is a cold-climate failure mode.** The mechanism depends on the engine taking a long time to reach operating temperature, so unburnt fuel does not evaporate and get routed back through the crankcase ventilation system. Short trips in genuinely cold weather are the trigger. We flagged exactly this on the [Civic FC's 1.5 turbo](/blog/honda-civic-fc-pms-guide): PH sources report it as rare here, and **there is no HCPI recall or bulletin for it.**

**Second, and more decisive: the American warranty extension named 2017–2018 CR-V models — and the Philippine CR-V of that era did not have that engine.** The US fifth-generation CR-V got the 1.5 turbo. **Ours got a 2.0 naturally-aspirated petrol and a 1.6 diesel.** The 1.5 VTEC Turbo only arrived in a Philippine CR-V with the **sixth generation in September 2023.**

<Pullquote>The most widely-circulated CR-V engine problem on the internet describes an engine that no Philippine CR-V of that model year was ever fitted with.</Pullquote>

So if you are researching a 2018 CR-V to buy here, the US oil-dilution complaints, NHTSA counts and reliability scores for that year are **about a different vehicle with a different engine.** This is the same trap we flagged on the [Ford Territory](/blog/ford-territory-pms-guide), whose 1.5 is a China-built JMC unit rather than the European Ford engine every forum answer discusses. **A shared badge and a shared model year are not a shared vehicle.** Filter your research by market before you let it price a car.

## Buying an Old One: Why They're Still Everywhere

You notice this yourself if you drive here: **old CR-Vs are still on the road in numbers**, including first- and second-generation cars that are now twenty to thirty years old. There is a mechanical reason for that, and it also explains the quiet interest they have picked up among enthusiasts.

**A first-generation CR-V is a B-series Honda.** The **B20B** is the same engine family as the **B16A2** in the [Civic SiR](/blog/honda-civic-sir-guide) — and among Honda builders the B20 block has a specific value: it is the large-displacement block of choice for **B20/VTEC** builds, where the block is paired with a VTEC cylinder head. Second-generation cars are **K-series**, and the **K24** is one of the most sought-after four-cylinder swap blocks anywhere in the world.

That has a real market consequence: **old CR-Vs are worth something as donors, not just as transport.** A tidy RD1 competes for buyers with people who want the engine.

<Pullquote>Part of why old CR-Vs hold their value here is that some of the buyers do not want a CR-V. They want the block.</Pullquote>

**A necessary honesty note:** what we are describing is a pattern visible in local listings and in Honda-builder circles, not a documented trend with survey data behind it. Treat it as a well-grounded observation about why these cars are hard to find cheap, not as a market forecast.

**Second-hand pricing, as observed in September 2026:** listing sites carry pre-owned CR-Vs from roughly **₱220,000** for an early-2000s 2.4 automatic, with the broader used inventory generally starting around **₱348,000** and a well-kept 2003 with 172,000 km asking in the **₱368,000–₱428,000** range. **These are asking prices on listing sites, not valuations** — condition, mileage, papers and provenance move them enormously, and a listing price is where negotiation starts.

**What to actually inspect on an old CR-V:**

- **Timing belt records on any gen-1 B20B.** Belt and water pump together, and age matters more than mileage. No records means budget for the job.
- **Rear differential Dual Pump Fluid on any 4WD or AWD unit.** Test it in a car park: tight, slow turns should be silent and smooth. Noise or binding is a finding.
- **Rust** — rear wheel arches, tailgate lower edge, and the floor under the carpets. This is where a thirty-year-old body in this climate tells the truth.
- **Automatic transmission behaviour when cold**, on the drive, not after the seller has warmed it up for you.
- **Papers and chassis number** against the physical stamping. On a car old enough to have passed through many hands, this is not paranoia — it is the whole due-diligence exercise.

## Casa vs. Talyer

Under warranty, this one is less of a judgment call than usual — and the reason is the warranty itself.

**Honda Secure Plus, launched May 2026, extends coverage from 3 years / 100,000 km to 5 years / 150,000 km at no additional cost** on the **Prelude** and the three **2026-model-year CR-V variants** (2.0 RS e:HEV AWD E-CVT, 2.0 VX e:HEV E-CVT, and 1.5 V Turbo HuNT CVT). Two years and 50,000 km of extra coverage for free is a genuinely strong offer, and worth reading precisely:

- **It is conditional on the PMS schedule.** Eligibility requires strictly following the prescribed Periodic Maintenance Service at **any authorised Honda Cars dealership.** Skip services or go to a talyer during the covered period and you are gambling with the extension, not just the paperwork.
- **It is not retroactive.** A **2023, 2024 or 2025** CR-V is on the standard **3 years / 100,000 km.** Do not let a listing for a used gen-6 imply otherwise.
- **The hybrid battery is separate: 8 years or 160,000 km**, and Secure Plus did not change it.

**One discrepancy we are not going to paper over.** The 2026 CR-V materials and the Secure Plus announcement both state the hybrid battery warranty as **8 years / 160,000 km.** Local coverage of the *earlier* RS e:HEV has also carried an **8 years / 200,000 km** figure — a number we cited ourselves in the [HR-V guide](/blog/honda-hr-v-pms-guide) as originating with the CR-V. We cannot reconcile the two from published sources, and we are not going to pick the one that reads better. **Get the exact battery terms for your specific model year written into your paperwork.** And regardless of the number: **a battery warranty is worth nothing without a documented service history**, so keep every receipt.

Once out of warranty, the split is the usual one with two CR-V-specific exceptions:

- **The CVT on the V Turbo** is where casa work pays for itself, purely on the HCF-2 question.
- **The hybrid system on the e:HEV** is casa work, full stop — high-voltage components, Honda-specific diagnostics, and a battery warranty that depends on documented history.
- **The AWD rear differential** needs the correct Honda fluid wherever you have it done. A good independent will do it properly if you hand them the right fluid; most will not have it on the shelf.

Everything else — oil, brakes, filters, tyres, suspension — a competent independent handles fine. Our [breakdown of when the casa is worth the premium and when it isn't](/blog/casa-vs-talyer-philippines) goes through the trade-off in general terms.

One structural point specific to Honda: **38 dealerships and three service centres nationwide** is materially thinner than Toyota's or Mitsubishi's network. Check what is actually near you before buying — and weigh it much more heavily if you are buying the hybrid, because that is the variant you cannot take just anywhere.

## How the CR-V Compares

- **Versus the [Toyota Fortuner](/blog/toyota-fortuner-pms-guide) and [Mitsubishi Montero Sport](/blog/mitsubishi-montero-sport-pms-guide):** different vehicles pretending to be rivals. Those are body-on-frame diesels with real towing and rough-road ability, on a 5,000 km service cycle. The CR-V is a monocoque crossover with half the service visits, a better ASEAN NCAP result, and a hybrid option neither offers. If your use case involves provincial roads and floods, they win. If it involves the metro, the CR-V is the better-mannered choice.
- **Versus the [Toyota Corolla Cross](/blog/toyota-corolla-cross-pms-guide):** the closest hybrid fight, one segment down and considerably cheaper. Toyota's hybrid has far more years on Philippine roads and a much denser dealer network. The CR-V answers with size, equipment and a stronger safety score.
- **Versus the [Innova Zenix](/blog/toyota-innova-zenix-pms-guide):** the direct answer to the CR-V's biggest gap. If you want a hybrid with seven seats, Toyota sells one and Honda does not.
- **Versus its own siblings, the [HR-V](/blog/honda-hr-v-pms-guide) and [BR-V](/blog/honda-br-v-pms-guide):** same 10,000 km interval, same brand advantages, much less money. The BR-V gives you seven seats for well under half the CR-V's price. The CR-V's case over both is size, refinement, the turbo engine and the 2.0 hybrid — not maintenance economics, which are broadly similar.

## Who This Car Is Actually For

**Buy the 1.5 V Turbo** if you want the CR-V's space and the only third row in the range, and you accept being in the entry variant. It is the value pick of the lineup by a clear margin, and the seven seats are a genuine capability the pricier variants no longer have.

**Buy the VX e:HEV** if you do mostly city driving and want the hybrid's fuel and brake-wear savings without paying for AWD you will not use. Price the premium over the V Turbo against your actual annual fuel spend — on low mileage, the arithmetic may not close.

**Buy the RS e:HEV AWD** if you want the full car and will use the all-wheel drive. Do the rear differential fluid on schedule, and get the battery warranty terms in writing.

**Think harder if** you need seven usable seats for adults, tow anything, live far from a Honda dealer, or are choosing the hybrid specifically — thirty-eight dealerships is a real constraint on a high-voltage vehicle.

**And if you are shopping used:** a **third- or fourth-generation** car (2007–2016) is the least dramatic CR-V you can own — chain-driven, mechanically simple, parts everywhere. A **first-generation** car is a project with a genuinely interesting engine in it. A **1.6 diesel** is a good car with one specific appetite you have to feed: open-road running.

## The Bottom Line

The CR-V is an easy car to own on a calendar that asks half as much of you as its diesel rivals: **10,000 km or six months, timing chain, no belt job waiting** — unless you are in a first-generation car, where the belt is the whole conversation. It carries **5 stars and 87.16 points at ASEAN NCAP** with a near-perfect adult-protection score, and the 2026 model year comes with **five years of warranty at no extra cost**, provided you actually keep the PMS at a dealer.

The things worth knowing before you sign are the ones the brochure does not lead with. **The only turbo is the only seven-seater, and it is the cheapest variant.** The **e:HEV's "E-CVT" is not a CVT**, and the V Turbo's CVT needs **HCF-2 and nothing else.** Every **AWD** unit has a rear differential quietly waiting for a fluid change nobody scheduled. The **hybrid battery warranty is quoted at two different figures**, so get yours in writing. **Honda's own cost calculator has now been offline for months**, which leaves a written dealer quote as your only real number. And the internet's favourite CR-V engine problem describes **an engine no Philippine CR-V of that era ever had.**

Run your VIN through Honda's recall tool, get the CVT and differential intervals written into your booklet, and pick the variant on the seats-versus-drivetrain trade-off rather than on trim level. Do that and this is one of the most straightforward large vehicles you can own here — which, thirty years in, is roughly what the CR-V has always been.
