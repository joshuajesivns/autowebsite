Every market needs a cheapest new car. In the Philippines, that job belongs to the **Suzuki S-Presso** — ₱634,000, five seats, a warranty, and a shape that pretends to be a tiny SUV without actually being one.

Cars at this price get judged unfairly in both directions. Enthusiasts write them off as tin cans; the marketing writes them up as clever urban mobility. Neither is much use if you're the person actually deciding whether to spend ₱634,000 of your own money — probably your **first ever brand-new car**.

So this guide does something different. It answers the three questions the S-Presso actually raises, honestly: **what does it cost to keep**, **what is an AGS gearbox really**, and **is the zero-star crash test thing true**. The third one has a genuine answer that almost nobody online gets right.

## Key Takeaways

- **Suzuki Philippines publishes neither a PMS price list nor a maintenance schedule** for its passenger cars. We checked the warranty page, the service page and the campaign portal. This is a real gap — Mitsubishi publishes a full per-model checksheet.
- **The service interval is genuinely ambiguous**: Suzuki PH's only published cadence (the Carry) is 5,000 km / 4 months, while the Indian documentation for *this exact car* says 10,000 km / 1 year. Your booklet is the authority — and the difference is double the visits over the car's life.
- **AGS is not an automatic.** It's a robotised manual with a real clutch, which is a wear item. The jerkiness is inherent, not a fault.
- **The "zero-star" story is about a spec the Philippines doesn't get.** That car had one airbag. The dual-airbag version scored **3 stars** for adults. But **child protection is weak in both** — no ISOFIX, no three-point belts in all seats.
- **There's a steering tie-rod recall you should check.** 87,599 India-built units from the July 2021–Feb 2023 window. PH cars come from that same plant.
- **Timing chain**, so no scheduled belt bill — but it's an interference engine, so oil discipline is the whole game.

## Which Model Years Does This Guide Cover? (2020–2026)

There is one important split in the S-Presso's Philippine life, and it's not cosmetic — it's the engine.

**March 14, 2020 — PH launch.** Introduced as the cheapest car in the Suzuki Philippines range, sitting below the Celerio. At launch it was **manual only**, with the older **K10B** engine.

**April–May 2023 — the update that matters.** Suzuki Philippines brought in the **AGS (Auto Gear Shift)** variant (arriving 15 April, launched 15 May 2023), and with it the **new K10C engine** with **Engine Auto Start Stop (EASS)**. So a 2023-or-later car is a meaningfully different machine from a 2020–2022 one: different engine, and the option of two pedals.

**Current 2026 lineup:** two variants — **GL MT at ₱634,000** and **GL AGS at ₱674,000**. A "Special Edition" existed and has been discontinued.

This guide is written around the **current K10C car**. If you own a 2020–2022 K10B S-Presso, the chassis, brakes, suspension and general ownership advice all still hold, but treat the engine-specific details as "check your own booklet."

## The Service Schedule — and Why It's Not a Simple Answer

Here's where we have to be straight with you, because this is the most important thing in this guide and the answer is genuinely unclear.

**Suzuki Philippines does not publish a maintenance schedule for its passenger cars.** We checked their warranty page, their service page, and their service-campaign portal. There is no per-model schedule and no PMS price list anywhere on their site.

For contrast: when we researched the [Mitsubishi L300](/blog/mitsubishi-l300-pms-guide), Mitsubishi Philippines had a full, item-by-item PMS checksheet published as a downloadable document. Suzuki has nothing equivalent. That's not a small difference — it means an S-Presso owner has to take a service adviser's word for what's due.

What we *can* establish, and the tension inside it:

| Source | Stated interval |
|---|---|
| **Suzuki PH — official, but for the Carry** | **Every 4 months or 5,000 km**, whichever comes first |
| **Indian documentation — for this exact car** | **Every 10,000 km or 1 year** |
| Suzuki PH, S-Presso specifically | *Not published — 1 year of free PMS is included* |

That gap matters more than it looks. Over 100,000 km, a 5,000 km interval means **twenty oil services**; a 10,000 km interval means **ten**. That's a material difference in what the car costs you, and it's worth knowing which one you actually signed up for.

**Your Warranty and Service Record Booklet is the authority.** Find it, read the schedule page, and keep it. Two honest points about the tension:

1. **The Indian figure is unusually relevant here** — the PH S-Presso isn't a locally-assembled variant, it's the Indian car, shipped whole from the same plant. So the Indian factory schedule isn't a foreign curiosity; it may well be the schedule the car was designed around.
2. **But shorter intervals are defensible in the Philippines.** Dust, heat, humidity, stop-start traffic and fuel quality all shorten oil life, and most PH dealers work on a 5,000 km rhythm for good reason. Servicing more often than the book says won't hurt the car — it just costs more.

What you shouldn't accept is not knowing. **Ask to be shown the schedule in your own booklet**, and if a shop tells you something is due, ask which interval it's due at.

## Light vs. Heavy (Major) PMS

Without a published sheet we won't fake a per-interval table. But the shape of servicing on a small naturally-aspirated three-cylinder is well established, and it's genuinely simple — one of the real arguments for this car.

**Light PMS** — the routine visit:
- Engine oil and oil filter
- Air cleaner element inspection and cleaning
- Coolant and brake fluid level checks, drive belt inspection
- Brake pads, discs and rear drums inspected
- Tyre rotation, pressures, condition
- **Clutch inspection** — worth calling out on AGS cars specifically
- Battery check

**Heavy / major stops** — where the bills cluster:
- **Air cleaner element replacement**
- **Spark plugs** — note there are **three**, not four. A small, real saving every time.
- **Coolant replacement**
- **Brake fluid replacement**
- **Manual transaxle service** — the same gearbox in MT and AGS cars
- Suspension, steering linkage and driveshaft boot inspection
- Aircon evaporator clean and filter replacement

The honest summary: **there is very little on this car to go expensively wrong at a service.** No turbo, no CVT, no direct injection, no diesel after-treatment, no hybrid battery, three spark plugs instead of four. Small car, small bills. That is the entire point of it, and it's a real one.

## The AGS Question — What You're Actually Buying

This is the single most misunderstood thing about the S-Presso, and it costs people money.

**AGS is not an automatic transmission.** It's Suzuki's **Auto Gear Shift** — a conventional 5-speed manual gearbox with **electro-hydraulic actuators operating the clutch and selecting the gears** for you. There's no clutch pedal and no shifting to do, but underneath it is a manual, all the way down.

What follows from that, practically:

<Split image={img1} side="right">
**The jerkiness is inherent, not a defect.** Every AMT pauses at the shift, because a real clutch has to physically disengage and re-engage. If yours nods your head slightly on upshifts, that isn't a fault to chase — it's how the mechanism works. Lifting off the throttle slightly as it shifts smooths it out noticeably.

**The clutch is a wear item.** This is the one that matters. Heavy stop-start crawling is the hardest life you can give an AMT clutch, and Metro Manila traffic is exactly that.
</Split>

**The upside is real too, and it's usually undersold.** Because there's a manual gearbox inside, an AGS is **mechanically simpler and cheaper to fix than a CVT** if something major ever goes wrong, and it doesn't have a CVT's belt or fluid-condition anxieties. It's also more fuel-efficient than a torque-converter automatic of the same era. For a first automatic car at this price, that's a sensible trade.

**What we deliberately won't claim:** you'll find sites quoting a specific AGS fluid interval, specific clutch lifespans in kilometres, and repair costs in Indian rupees, all attributed to audits we could not verify. We're not repeating those. Ask your dealer for the AGS service requirements in writing, and treat anything you read with a rupee sign as not applicable here.

**Practical advice if you buy the AGS:** use manual mode on steep climbs rather than letting it hunt, come to a complete stop before selecting drive or reverse, and don't hold the car on the throttle on an incline — use the brake, and let Hill Hold do its job.

## The Safety Conversation, Handled Properly

The S-Presso carries a reputation as "the zero-star car." That reputation comes from a real test, and it's being applied to the wrong vehicle. Here is the actual record.

| Test | Spec tested | Adult | Child |
|---|---|---|---|
| Global NCAP, 2020 | **Indian-spec, driver airbag only** | **0 stars** | 2 stars |
| Global NCAP + AA South Africa, 2022 | **India-built, dual front airbags** | **3 stars** | 15 / 49 points (low) |

**The Philippine-market S-Presso has dual airbags, ABS and ESP as standard** — that's from Suzuki Philippines' own specification page. On airbag count, our car matches the configuration that scored **3 stars**, not the one that scored zero. So "the S-Presso is a zero-star car" is, for a Philippine buyer, **not an accurate statement.**

But this is not a clean win, and it would be dishonest to present it as one:

- **3 stars is still 3 stars.** It's a modest result, not a good one.
- **Child occupant protection is weak in both tested versions**, and the reason is structural rather than fixable by spec: Global NCAP flagged **no ISOFIX anchorages and no three-point seatbelts in all seating positions**. We found no ISOFIX listed for the PH car either. **If you regularly carry a small child, this is the number that should actually influence your decision** — not the zero-star headline.
- The 2022 test predates the PH car's current ESP fitment, so our car may well do better than 3 stars — **but nobody has tested it, so that's a hope, not a result.**

The fair conclusion: the S-Presso is **better than its internet reputation and still the weakest link in its own spec sheet.** At ₱634,000 that's a trade some buyers will rationally make. Make it knowingly.

## Known Issues

Only what we could verify. We're not padding this section.

### The recall you should actually check

**Steering tie rod — Maruti Suzuki India, announced 24 July 2023.** **87,599 units** of the **S-Presso and Eeco**, built between **5 July 2021 and 15 February 2023**, recalled because of a suspected defect in part of the **steering tie rod** which "in a rare case, may break and affect vehicle steerability and handling." Inspection and replacement were free.

**Why this matters to a Philippine owner:** the PH S-Presso is a **CBU import from Manesar, India** — the same production source. A PH-market car built inside that window plausibly shares the affected part. **We could not confirm whether Suzuki Philippines ran a matching campaign** — their service-campaign portal blocked our access — so this is "we couldn't verify," not "there's no PH campaign."

**If your unit is from that build window, have your VIN checked.** An open recall is free work; the thing to worry about is a seller who doesn't know about it.

### Verified owner-reported issues

These come up consistently enough across Philippine reviews and owner feedback to state as real:

- **NVH is the headline complaint.** The three-cylinder is audible and vibrates noticeably at idle, and cabin insulation is thin enough that owners describe road noise as sounding like a window left open. This is the most common criticism by a wide margin.
- **Ride quality over broken surfaces.** At ~770 kg there's very little mass to absorb impacts, so poor roads come through clearly, and there's noticeable body roll if you push a corner.
- **Crosswind sensitivity.** The tall, slab-sided shape gets pushed around at expressway speeds. Worth knowing before a long provincial trip.
- **Sparse equipment**, honestly reflecting the price: no rear speakers, manual rear windows, manually adjusted mirrors.
- **Centre-mounted instrument cluster** — a genuine and persistent complaint, though a preference issue rather than a defect.
- **Build-quality complaints exist** in owner reviews but are a minority position — aggregate owner sentiment on the PH review sites runs strongly positive (on one site, 61 of 66 owners happy).

**Fuel economy, measured:** one Philippine road test recorded **17 km/L in urban driving** — though under unusually light pandemic-era traffic, with the reviewer expecting **around 12 km/L** in normal conditions. Treat 12 km/L as the realistic city figure.

**What we did not find:** any documented widespread mechanical failure pattern on the engine or gearbox. The K10 is a very high-volume, long-established engine family, and Philippine coverage of "common problems" for this car is notably thin on actual mechanical faults — which is itself a reasonable signal.

## Casa vs. Talyer, and the Parts Question

**Stay at the casa for the first year** — the free PMS makes it a non-decision — and through the 3-year/100,000 km warranty for anything that could plausibly become a claim.

After that, the mechanical side is easy: the **K10 is one of the most-produced small engines in the world**, shared with the Celerio and built in the millions. Filters, plugs, pads, belts and fluids are ordinary parts at ordinary prices, and there's nothing exotic for an independent shop to be scared of.

**The genuine parts risk isn't consumables — it's collision and trim.** Because the S-Presso is a low-volume CBU import rather than a locally-assembled bestseller like the Vios or Wigo, **body panels, glass, trim pieces and model-specific electronics can mean waiting on an order.** If you have a bad shunt, that's where the pain lands. The **AGS actuator** is likewise a dealer-level part, not something a corner shop stocks.

Our [breakdown of dealer versus independent servicing](/blog/casa-vs-talyer-philippines) covers how to make that switch without hurting resale. One S-Presso-specific note: **AGS work is not general-talyer work.** The gearbox is a manual any shop can handle, but the actuators and their software are not.

## Who This Car Is Actually For

Being clear about this is more useful than a verdict.

**Buy it if:** you want the cheapest brand-new car with a real warranty; your driving is overwhelmingly urban and short-haul; you park in tight spaces (that 4.5 m turning circle is genuinely excellent); you need the 180 mm of ground clearance for flood-prone roads; and fuel and service costs matter more to you than refinement.

**Don't buy it if:** you regularly do long expressway runs with a full car; you carry small children often (see the child-protection section); or you'd find the noise and vibration wearing on a daily basis — because that complaint doesn't fade with familiarity.

**The honest cross-shop** is the [Toyota Wigo and its running costs](/blog/toyota-wigo-pms-guide) — better refined and backed by the deepest dealer and parts network in the country, but it asks more money and gives up the S-Presso's ground clearance. The other Suzuki worth a look is the [Dzire, also an India-built CBU](/blog/suzuki-dzire-pms-guide), if you'd rather have a small sedan with a boot than a tall hatch.

## The Bottom Line

The S-Presso does the job it was built for. **₱634,000, three years of warranty, a year of free PMS, a timing chain, three spark plugs, 180 mm of clearance and very little to go expensively wrong.** For someone buying their first new car, that combination is worth taking seriously, and the mechanical simplicity is a genuine long-term asset rather than a marketing line.

What to actually hold on to:

1. **Find your Warranty and Service Record Booklet and read the schedule.** Suzuki PH publishes nothing, and the two available reference points differ by a factor of two.
2. **Check your VIN against the July 2021–Feb 2023 tie-rod recall** if your car falls in that window.
3. **Understand that AGS is a robotised manual** — the jerk is normal, the clutch is a wear item, and heavy traffic is what kills it.
4. **The zero-star story isn't our car** — but weak child protection is, and that's the number that should move your decision.
5. **Budget for collision parts, not service parts.** The routine stuff is cheap; the imported panels are not.

At si S-Presso naman, hindi siya nagpapanggap. **Mura siya, matipid siya, at simple siya** — at sa segment na 'to, ang katapatan sa presyo ay isang klase rin ng respeto.
