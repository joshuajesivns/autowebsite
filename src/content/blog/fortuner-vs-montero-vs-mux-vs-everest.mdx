If you are buying a midsize diesel SUV in the Philippines, four names make every shortlist: the **Toyota Fortuner**, **Mitsubishi Montero Sport**, **Isuzu mu-X**, and **Ford Everest**. Body-on-frame, seven seats, turbo-diesel, built to take a family from EDSA to the province and back.

On paper they look interchangeable. In ownership they are not — and the difference that costs you the most money is the one nobody puts on a brochure: **how often each of them makes you go to the casa.** One of these four asks for twice as many service visits as the others, and it is the best-selling one.

This is the honest head-to-head, current as of **September 2026** — which matters more than usual this month, because Ford revamped the entire Everest lineup days before we published. This is the diesel-SUV companion to our [four-way pickup comparison](/blog/hilux-vs-ranger-vs-dmax-vs-navara) and our [complete guide to PMS schedules and cost](/blog/car-pms-philippines-guide).

## Key Takeaways

- **The Fortuner services twice as often as the rest.** Toyota runs it at **5,000 km / 6 months**; the mu-X and Everest both go **10,000 km**. At 20,000 km a year that is **four casa visits against two.**
- **The Montero Sport's interval depends on your model year**, and Mitsubishi publishes two different checksheets for it. The **25MY (2025+)** runs 10,000 km / 6 months. A **2016–2024** car runs **5,000 km / 4 months on mineral oil**, stretching to 10,000 km / 8 months only on fully synthetic.
- **The Everest's wet belt may be gone — but nobody has confirmed it.** Ford's 2.0 diesel was reported to switch to a timing chain in the first half of 2026, and the new PH lineup matches that description exactly. Ford PH publishes no timing spec. **Get it in writing.**
- **Ford revamped the PH Everest in early September 2026** — bi-turbo dropped, all 10-speed automatic, new **Active** entry at ₱1,949,000, and a new **Platinum 3.0 V6** at ₱2,849,000 with 247 hp / 600 Nm.
- **The Montero Sport is the cheapest way in** at **₱1,568,000**; the Everest Platinum is the most expensive at **₱2,849,000**.
- **Warranty: Everest 5 yr / 150,000 km** leads, Fortuner is effectively 5 yr / 140,000 km with the loyalty extension, and **Montero Sport and mu-X are both 3 yr / 100,000 km** — the shortest.
- **Timing chain is confirmed on three of the four**: Fortuner (2.4 and 2.8), Montero Sport (4N15), and mu-X (both 1.9 and 3.0). Only the Everest's answer is complicated.
- **The Fortuner still wins resale**, and in this segment that quietly pays back a lot of the higher service frequency.

## Meet the Four

**Toyota Fortuner** — the segment default. Current generation dates to 2016 with an October 2020 facelift; **there is no all-new Fortuner for 2026**, despite the all-new Hilux that arrived in June. Nine variants, 2.4L or 2.8L diesel, and the strongest resale in the class.

**Mitsubishi Montero Sport** — the value play and the comfort pick. One engine, four variants, an 8-speed automatic that is genuinely the smoothest here, and the lowest entry price of the four.

**Isuzu mu-X** — the low-drama choice. Shares its engines and much of its hardware with the [D-Max pickup](/blog/isuzu-d-max-pms-guide): timing chain on both engines, a 10,000 km interval, and two free services to start. Its weak point is warranty length.

**Ford Everest** — the tech and power pick, and the one that just changed. As of early September 2026 the PH lineup is all 10-speed automatic, the bi-turbo is gone, and a 3.0 V6 sits on top for the first time.

Two things worth spelling out.

**The Fortuner's 5,000 km interval is the real cost difference.** Its per-visit price is comparable to the Montero Sport's — roughly ₱8,000–12,000 for a light PMS at the casa — but it wants that visit twice as often as an mu-X or Everest. Over 100,000 km that is **20 oil changes against 10**. Against its resale advantage, it is close to a wash; but you should know you are making the trade.

**The Montero Sport's schedule genuinely depends on which oil the casa pours.** Mitsubishi Philippines publishes two separate Periodic Maintenance Service Guides for this car, one headed *24MY and older* and one headed *25MY*, and they are not the same document. On the older sheet the oil interval is conditional: **5,000 km / 4 months on mineral, 10,000 km / 8 months on fully synthetic.** Most owners never get asked which one they want. Ask. We covered the full item-by-item split in the [Montero Sport PMS guide](/blog/mitsubishi-montero-sport-pms-guide).

## The Timing Question: Chain, Wet Belt, and What Just Changed

In our [pickup comparison](/blog/hilux-vs-ranger-vs-dmax-vs-navara) this was the cleanest line in the whole piece: three chains and one wet belt. Here it is messier, and being honest about the mess is more useful than pretending it is simple.

**Confirmed timing chain, no scheduled replacement:**

- **Toyota Fortuner** — both the 2.4L 2GD-FTV and 2.8L 1GD-FTV.
- **Mitsubishi Montero Sport** — the 2.4L 4N15.
- **Isuzu mu-X** — both the 1.9L RZ4E-TC and 3.0L 4JJ3-TCX.

**The Ford Everest is where it gets complicated**, in two separate ways.

The **2.0L EcoBlue** has always used a **"wet" timing belt** — a rubber belt running submerged in engine oil. It is the most argued-about part of this engine, and the reason is real: Ford revised the belt's service interval downward in other markets, from roughly 10 years / 240,000 km to **6 years / 160,000 km**, after reports of premature degradation linked to oil dilution. Those revisions are documented for EcoBlue engines in the UK and for the Transit; **we could not verify any Everest-specific wet-belt interval published by Ford Philippines.**

Then in the first half of 2026, Chasing Cars reported that Ford **switched the 2.0 four-cylinder diesel from the wet belt to a conventional timing chain**, and discontinued the bi-turbo globally in favour of a single-turbo with new injectors and the 10-speed automatic. **Ford did not officially confirm the timing change.**

Here is why that matters to you right now: **the PH lineup Ford launched in early September 2026 matches that description exactly** — bi-turbo gone, 10-speed across the board, the 2.0 quoted at 168 hp / 405 Nm, which is precisely the single-turbo output in the report. That is strong circumstantial evidence the PH car is now on a chain. It is not confirmation, and we are not going to print it as one.

The **3.0L V6** in the new Platinum does not solve the problem by being bigger. Ford's own technical documentation for the 3.0 Power Stroke describes a **cogged timing belt driving the exhaust camshafts, with cam-to-cam chains** linking each exhaust cam to its intake cam. So the V6 is belt-driven too, in its own way, and we found no PH-published replacement interval for it either.

**What to do about it:** if you are buying an Everest, ask the dealer to confirm **in writing** whether your specific unit's engine uses a chain or a belt, and what the published replacement interval is. And remember the point from the warranty section — **a timing belt is scheduled maintenance, not a defect**, so no warranty length protects you from its cost.

## Fuel Economy

We are deliberately not publishing a four-way fuel-economy table here, because **we could not verify apples-to-apples figures for all four tested under comparable conditions**, and a table implying that precision would be dishonest.

What we can say with confidence: the **mu-X's 3.0L 4JJ3 has the strongest documented economy record** of these engines, through its shared history with the D-Max, which holds AAP-certified eco-run results above 20 km/L. Treat that as a controlled-conditions ceiling, not a daily number.

The more reliable saving is structural rather than mechanical: **a 10,000 km interval saves you real money whether or not the engine sips less fuel.**

## Safety & Tech

All four carry **5-star ASEAN NCAP ratings**. We are not going to rank them by score, because those tests were conducted in different years under **different ASEAN NCAP protocols** — the scoring changed in 2017 and again afterwards — so the raw numbers are not comparable, and a league table built from them would be fiction dressed as data.

The comparison that is still meaningful is the driver-assist suite: **Toyota Safety Sense** on the Fortuner, **Mitsubishi Motors Safety Sensing** on the Montero Sport, **ADAS on the higher LS-E** mu-X trims, and **Ford Co-Pilot360** on the Everest. Check the variant, not the brochure — none of these are uniformly standard across every trim.

## Warranty, Dealer Network & Resale

| Model | Standard warranty | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Ford Everest** | **5 years / 150,000 km** | Longest here; time-limited 5-Star Care Package bundles service and roadside |
| **Toyota Fortuner** | 3 yr / 100,000 km + loyalty extension = **effectively 5 yr / 140,000 km** | The extension requires on-time casa PMS — miss them and it lapses |
| **Mitsubishi Montero Sport** | **3 years / 100,000 km** | Shortest, tied |
| **Isuzu mu-X** | **3 years / 100,000 km** | Shortest, tied — but starts with two free services |

Two honest qualifications. The Fortuner's headline five years is **conditional** — it is a loyalty extension you earn by servicing on time at the casa, on a car that wants servicing twice as often as its rivals. And the Everest's category-leading warranty **does not cover the timing job**, because scheduled maintenance is not a defect.

On network and resale, **Toyota wins both and it is not close.** The dealer footprint is the largest in the country, and the Fortuner holds the strongest residuals in the segment — the same pattern the Hilux shows among pickups. If you change cars every five years, that residual advantage can quietly repay the extra service visits.

## Which Should You Buy?

- **Buy the Fortuner** if resale, dealer reach and the safety of the obvious choice matter most — and you accept four casa visits a year.
- **Buy the Montero Sport** if you want the most SUV for the least money and the smoothest drivetrain here. If it is a 2016–2024 unit, **ask for fully synthetic** and halve your service visits.
- **Buy the mu-X** if you want the fewest unknowns: chain on both engines, a 10,000 km interval, two free services. Accept the 3-year warranty.
- **Buy the Everest** if you want the most modern car and the most power, especially the new V6 — and you are willing to pin the dealer down on the timing question first.

## The Verdict

For most Filipino families, this comes down to **Fortuner or mu-X**, for opposite reasons. The Fortuner is the safest ownership decision in the country and pays you back when you sell it. The mu-X is the cheapest and least eventful to actually run.

The **Montero Sport** is the value pick and deserves more attention than it gets, especially at ₱1,568,000. The **Everest** is the most car here and the most interesting it has been in years — and also the one with the most unanswered questions, all of which a dealer can settle in five minutes if you ask.

The one thing we would not do is choose on sticker price alone. **The difference between a 5,000 km and a 10,000 km service interval, over ten years of ownership, is larger than most of the price gaps in that table.**
