The **Mitsubishi Xforce** arrived in the Philippines in 2024 as the brand's answer to a segment that had gotten crowded fast — the subcompact crossover, where the Raize, Yaris Cross and Corolla Cross were already fighting over the same buyer. It looks sharper than most of them, it is built on the same bones as the country's best-selling MPV, and it undercuts a lot of what it parks beside.

But the thing worth knowing before you sign anything is not on the spec sheet the salesman hands you. **Mitsubishi Motors Philippines' own Service Guide puts the Xforce on a 10,000 km / 6-month maintenance cycle — not the 5,000 km cycle the Xpander runs.** The Xforce is listed there in the same group as the Triton and the 25MY Montero Sport, and that single line changes the ownership arithmetic more than any accessory package will.

Here's the real schedule straight off that document, what each stop covers, the CVT question, and an honest account of what it costs. This is the Xforce companion to our [complete guide to PMS meaning, schedule and cost](/blog/car-pms-philippines-guide).

## Key Takeaways

- **PMS every 10,000 km or 6 months**, per Mitsubishi PH's published Service Guide — **half as many casa visits as an Xpander**, which runs the 5,000 km cycle.
- A **light PMS** should land around **₱5,000–7,500**; the **heavy service at 40,000 km** nearer **₱10,000–16,000**. *(Estimates, August 2026 — MMPC publishes no per-model price list.)*
- **Timing chain, not belt.** The 4A91 has no scheduled timing-belt bill waiting at 100,000 km.
- **The CVT fluid is the line item to get right** — the Xforce is the only CVT vehicle in its section of Mitsubishi's schedule, so don't let a shop apply the Triton's automatic-transmission interval to it.
- **TPMS ID registration is part of every service.** Rotate the tyres anywhere else and the sensors need re-registering — an Xforce-specific row in the official sheet.
- **Warranty is 3 years / 100,000 km**, and it is conditional on servicing at an authorised dealer.

## Which Model Years Does This Guide Cover? (2024–2026)

The Xforce is a single generation so far, and it is genuinely new — which matters for how much you should trust anybody's "known issues" list, including ours.

Production started at Mitsubishi's **MMKI plant in Cikarang, West Java in October 2023**, with the first Indonesian customer deliveries on **19 November 2023**. The Philippine launch followed on **28 June 2024**, with customer deliveries starting **5 July 2024**. It is a CBU import from Indonesia, not locally assembled — the same origin as the [Destinator](/blog/mitsubishi-destinator-pms-guide).

At launch the Philippine range was two variants: **GLS at ₱1,367,000 and GT at ₱1,581,000**. At **PIMS in June 2026** Mitsubishi expanded the lineup downward with a new entry-level **GLX** and repositioned the prices. As of August 2026 Mitsubishi PH's own site lists:

| Variant | Price (MMPC, Aug 2026) |
|---|---|
| Xforce GLX 2WD CVT | ₱1,119,000 |
| Xforce GLS 2WD CVT | ₱1,299,000 |
| Xforce GT 2WD CVT | ₱1,661,000 |

The GLX also ran an introductory price of **₱1,049,000 until 31 July 2026**, which has now lapsed. Be aware that the major listing sites still show the older 2025 figures for the GLS and GT — if you are comparing prices online, check the date on the page. Mechanically, none of this changes the maintenance picture: **every Philippine Xforce is the same 1.5-litre naturally-aspirated engine with a CVT driving the front wheels**, so the schedule below applies across the range.

*Two notes on numbers you will see quoted elsewhere. **Ground clearance:** 193 mm is the minimum figure; the widely-shared **222 mm** is the global measurement to the metal underbody, not to the lowest point. Both are real, they just measure different things — use 193 mm when you are deciding whether to take a flooded street. **Power:** international sources list the 4A91 at up to 109 hp, but Mitsubishi Philippines' figure for this car is 105 PS / 141 Nm, and the local number is the one we use.*

**One spec we're deliberately leaving blank: oil capacity and grade.** The figures circulating for the 4A91 come mostly from generic engine-database sites, and those sites are exactly where we have been burned before — they routinely conflate engine variants across markets and production years. We could not confirm the Xforce's fill capacity or viscosity from a Mitsubishi Philippines document, so we are printing nothing rather than printing a number that sends you to the wrong oil. **Your owner's manual is the authority; ask the service adviser to show you the spec.**

## The Service Schedule — Straight From Mitsubishi's Own Guide

This is the part that makes this guide different from most of what you will find on the Xforce. For a lot of cars we have to reconstruct the schedule from owner reports. **For the Xforce we don't have to: Mitsubishi Motors Philippines publishes an actual Periodic Maintenance Service Guide, and the Xforce is named in it.**

The document splits Mitsubishi's Philippine range into two schedules. Most of the lineup — Mirage, Mirage G4, ASX and the rest — runs the familiar cycle of a **light PMS every 5,000 km or 3 months**, a regular service every 10,000 km, and a heavy service every 20,000 km. **The Xforce is not on that schedule.** It sits in the other group, alongside the Triton and the 25MY Montero Sport:

> **On the first 1,000 km → Every 10,000 km or 6 months interval → Every 20,000 km or 1 year interval**, with additional jobs at 40,000 km (2 years), 60,000 km (3 years), 80,000 km (4 years), 100,000 km (5 years) and 160,000 km (8 years).

Play that against an Xpander over a normal Philippine year:

| | Mitsubishi Xforce | Mitsubishi Xpander |
|---|---|---|
| Service interval | 10,000 km / 6 months | 5,000 km / 3–6 months |
| Casa visits per year (at 20,000 km) | **2** | **4** |
| Oil changes to 100,000 km | **10** | **20** |

### What actually gets done, interval by interval

Here is the official content of each stop, condensed to what applies to a front-wheel-drive petrol Xforce:

| Odometer | Service | What the guide lists |
|---|---|---|
| 1,000 km | First PMS | Oil and filter change, all-lights and switch check, cabin filter cleaned, fluid levels, battery status, radiator cap sticker, tyre condition |
| **Every 10,000 km / 6 mo** | **Light** | Oil and filter, cabin filter cleaned, air cleaner element checked, **brake pads/discs and rear shoes/drums checked and cleaned, tyres rotated, TPMS ID registration**, fluid levels, battery status |
| **Every 20,000 km / 1 yr** | **Bigger light** | Everything above **plus the cabin filter replaced** (not just cleaned), parking-brake stroke, suspension and steering-linkage check, body condition, road test on request |
| **40,000 km / 2 yr** | **Heavy / major** | Adds **air cleaner element replaced, brake and clutch fluid changed, drive belt checked, wheel alignment**, plus the fuel filter and CVT fluid items below |
| 60,000 km / 3 yr | Additional | Front and rear wheel bearings checked for play |
| 80,000 km / 4 yr | Additional | Second pass of the 40,000 km work |
| 100,000 km / 5 yr | Additional | **Spark plugs replaced** (iridium plugs are a 100,000 km item on Mitsubishi's schedule), valve clearance checked |

**Read the sheet as a superset, not a checklist.** It is written for three very different vehicles at once, so several rows simply do not apply to your car: the diesel fuel filter, the EGR and small-injection-quantity-learning items, the propeller shaft greasing and the 4WD transfer-case oil are all there for the Triton and Montero Sport. Your Xforce is a front-wheel-drive petrol crossover. If a quotation includes any of those, ask.

**And two rows we can't place with certainty, so we won't pretend otherwise.** The guide lists **Replace CVT fluid** and **Change engine coolant** in its additional-jobs block, but the table's column layout at that point is genuinely ambiguous — the CVT fluid row reads as part of the **40,000 km / 2-year** group, and the coolant row sits with the later intervals. For Philippine driving, 40,000 km is the safe assumption for the CVT fluid either way. Get both confirmed against your unit's manual before you plan a budget around them.

## Light vs. Heavy (Major) PMS

Same idea as every car on this site, but the spacing is different because the intervals are twice as long as the Mitsubishi cars most people are used to.

A **light PMS** — every 10,000 km — is the oil, the oil filter, a tyre rotation, and a structured inspection. It is the visit that keeps the engine healthy and catches problems while they are still cheap.

A **heavy or major PMS** is where parts get *replaced* instead of inspected: **engine air cleaner element, brake and clutch fluid, the CVT fluid, the fuel filter**, plus wheel alignment and a drive-belt check. On the Xforce this lands at **40,000 km**, and again at **80,000 km**.

Ang praktikal na payo: dahil malayo ang agwat ng bawat visit, madaling makalimutan na paparating na pala ang 40,000 km. Ipunan mo na — doble ang bill na iyon kumpara sa nakasanayan mong light PMS. At huwag mong i-stretch ang 6-month leg kahit mababa ang takbo mo: ang init, alikabok at traffic dito ay itinuturing na *severe service* ng manufacturer mismo.

## What It Actually Costs

We have to be straight about the sourcing, because it is the same wall every guide on this site runs into.

**Mitsubishi Motors Philippines publishes the schedule but not the prices.** They give you a detailed maintenance guide — genuinely more detailed than most brands here — and no per-model peso figure anywhere. That is now true of **Toyota, Honda, Isuzu, Suzuki, Nissan and Mitsubishi alike**: not one Philippine manufacturer publishes a per-model PMS price list. So anyone quoting you an exact casa price for an Xforce online is estimating, and they should say so.

One thing genuinely in the Xforce's favour: because it is a modest naturally-aspirated 1.5 with no turbo and no diesel aftertreatment, there is very little exotic on the bill. The heavy service is expensive because of the fluids and filters, not because anything complicated is being touched.

## The CVT, and the TPMS Detail Everyone Misses

<Split image={img2} side="right" alt="The Mitsubishi Xforce interior, showing the CVT gear selector, drive-mode dial and 12.3-inch touchscreen." caption="Every Xforce is a CVT — and it is the only CVT in its section of Mitsubishi's maintenance schedule.">

Every Philippine Xforce, in every variant, uses a CVT. Mitsubishi tunes it with what it describes as expanded ratios comparable to an 8-speed automatic, and reviewers generally rate the calibration well — CarGuide.PH noted the gearbox is "smart enough to hold its ratios to maximize its power."

The maintenance consequence is the important bit. **The Xforce is the only CVT vehicle in its section of Mitsubishi's Service Guide.** The Triton and Montero Sport it shares that schedule with are conventional automatics, which is why the sheet carries *both* a "Change automatic transmission fluid" row at 100,000 km and a separate "Replace CVT fluid" row.

**Those are not the same job and not the same interval.** If a shop reads the 100,000 km automatic-transmission line and applies it to your CVT, you will be running that fluid two and a half times longer than intended. A CVT's fluid is a working component — it transmits torque through a belt and pulleys under enormous clamping pressure — not just a lubricant.

</Split>

The other Xforce-specific line in the official guide is one almost nobody thinks about: **"Conduct TPMS ID registration (Xforce)"**, listed at every 10,000 km and 20,000 km service, right after the tyre rotation. The Xforce's tyre-pressure sensors are matched to positions, so when the wheels move, the system needs to be re-registered. It is routine at the casa. **It is not always routine at a vulcanising shop** — so if you rotate tyres or get a flat fixed outside the dealer and the TPMS light starts behaving strangely afterwards, this is usually why. Not a fault, just an unfinished job.

## Known Issues

**An honest framing first: this car is two years old in the Philippine market.** There is no meaningful long-term owner-defect record for it yet, and any article claiming a definitive list of Xforce reliability problems is padding. What exists so far is early owner sentiment plus professional road tests, and both are consistently positive on the mechanical side — no widespread failure pattern has surfaced in Philippine sources. That is worth stating plainly, because "we found nothing" is a finding, not a gap to fill with invented faults.

What *is* consistently reported is livability, not reliability:

- **The infotainment can lag.** This is the most repeated complaint from owners and reviewers alike — the touchscreen occasionally hangs or slows down, and CarGuide.PH flagged poor responsiveness in its GT review. Software, not mechanical. Ask your dealer whether a system update is available for your unit.
- **The engine runs out of breath when loaded.** 105 PS in a roughly 1,250 kg crossover is adequate, not generous. Reviewers were direct about this: performance falls off "as the speeds climb or the number of passengers increases," and overtaking with a full car takes planning. This is a buying consideration, not a defect — but drive it loaded before you commit.
- **Reviewers disagree about the ride, and it is worth knowing why.** Top Gear Philippines found the soft suspension "wallowy" at highway speed with noticeable body roll; CarGuide.PH found body lean "controlled" and the ride "well-sorted." Our read: it is a soft, comfort-biased setup, so how you feel about it depends on whether you value absorbing bad pavement or holding a line on the expressway. Judge it yourself on a real road.
- **Limited driving-position adjustment.** Top Gear noted limited steering-wheel adjustability and restricted front-passenger seat travel. If you are tall or short, sit in it properly before signing.
- **Trim quality is the weak spot to inspect.** Top Gear's test unit had a **loose A-pillar trim**. Isolated, but a fair thing to check on delivery.
- **The rear camera picks up rain splatter** from its placement — a minor irritation in the wet season, and one you will notice reversing.

On the other side of the ledger, the fuel economy is real: Top Gear Philippines measured **9.4 km/L in city traffic averaging 18 km/h and 17 km/L on the highway**, and CarGuide.PH recorded **10.41 km/L at a 16 km/h average**. For a 1,250 kg crossover in Manila traffic, those are good numbers.

### On recalls

**We could not find any recall or service campaign covering the Xforce in the Philippines as of August 2026.** We want to be precise about what that means: it means we didn't find one, not that none exists. Recall information here is scattered across manufacturer pages that are not always current, and we have seen campaigns exist without being easy to find. The VIN check is free — ask any Mitsubishi dealer, or check MMPC's Safety Inspection Campaign page. On a model this new, keep checking.

### On safety

For a car this fresh, the crash record is unusually solid. The Xforce earned **five stars in the 2024 ASEAN NCAP assessment with an overall score of 79.15**: **27.91 of 32.00** for Adult Occupant Protection, **43.43 of 51.00** for Child Occupant Protection, **15.59 of 21.00** for Safety Assist, and **9.91 of 16.00** for Motorcyclist Safety. That child-occupant number is the one worth pointing out — it is a strong result in a segment where child protection is often where the points get lost.

One caveat, and it is the same one that applies to every NCAP result: **the variant tested was the Indonesian Exceed.** The safety kit that comes with a Philippine GLX is not the same as a GT's. Check what is actually fitted to the variant you are buying rather than assuming the star rating transfers whole.

## Casa vs. Talyer, and Where to Draw the Line

The Xforce is a reasonable candidate for a mixed approach, with one firm exception.

Its routine consumables — oil, filters, plugs, brake pads — are ordinary. The 4A91 is a high-volume Mitsubishi engine shared with the Xpander, so a competent independent can source parts easily and price them below casa.

**Where we would hold the line: the CVT, and anything involving the TPMS or the driver-assist calibration.** The CVT wants the correct Mitsubishi fluid at the correct interval, and the electronics want equipment most general shops do not have. There is also the warranty argument, which on this car is not a small one — MMPC's guide states plainly that to keep your vehicle within warranty coverage, it should be checked only at authorised service centres. **You are 3 years or 100,000 km into that deal.** For a car still under warranty, the casa premium on scheduled PMS is usually the cheaper choice once you price in the risk.

For the general trade-off — what is safe to hand to a trusted mechanic and when — see our [casa versus talyer breakdown](/blog/casa-vs-talyer-philippines).

## Who This Car Is Actually For

The Xforce suits a buyer who wants a crossover that looks like it costs more than it does, drives mostly in the city, values a long service interval, and does not need seven seats or a turbo.

It is a weaker fit if you regularly run fully loaded on the expressway, if you want the segment's strongest engine, or if a laggy touchscreen is the kind of thing that will bother you every single day. Those are real trade-offs, and the reviewers who like this car say them out loud too.

## The Bottom Line

The Xforce's case is quieter than its styling suggests: **a naturally-aspirated engine with nothing exotic to fail, a timing chain, five-star crash protection, a temporary spare rather than a repair kit, and a service schedule that asks for half the casa visits of the Mitsubishi parked next to it.**

What to hold on to:

1. **Two services a year, not four.** Verify it against the Service Guide yourself — the Xforce is listed with the Triton and 25MY Montero Sport, not with the Mirage group.
2. **The CVT fluid is yours alone.** The 100,000 km automatic-transmission line on that sheet belongs to the Triton and Montero Sport. Don't let it get applied to your car.
3. **Budget for 40,000 km.** That is the heavy stop, and it arrives quietly when your visits are six months apart.
4. **Get the TPMS re-registered after any tyre work.** It is on the official checklist for a reason.
5. **Don't guess the oil.** Read it off your manual, not off a search result.

At sa huli: hindi ang pinakamabilis o pinakamalakas na crossover ang Xforce sa segment niya, at hindi rin naman siya nagpapanggap. **Ang totoong lakas niya ay nasa schedule** — mas kaunting pasok sa casa, mas simpleng makina, at walang sorpresang timing belt na aabangan. Para sa maraming Pilipinong bumibili ng unang crossover nila, tama lang ang kombinasyong iyon.
