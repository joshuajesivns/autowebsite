The Toyota Hiace is the **fifth best-selling vehicle in the Philippines** — 20,526 units in 2025, more than the Wigo, more than the Innova, more than the Fortuner. And unlike almost everything above it on that list, most Hiaces aren't family cars. They're **working assets**: shuttle vans, tourist transport, delivery fleets, school service, church vans, provincial routes.

That changes the maintenance question completely. For a family sedan, "magkano ang PMS?" is a budgeting question. For a Hiace, it's a **business input** — it sits next to fuel, driver salary, and downtime in the cost of every kilometer you sell. Miss the schedule and you don't just risk a repair bill, you lose earning days.

So this guide answers it the way an operator needs it answered: the real schedule, the real cost, the cost *per kilometer*, and the one warranty detail that quietly catches almost every high-mileage buyer.

## Key Takeaways

- **Two generations are on sale at the same time.** The cheap **Cargo and 3.0L Commuter are still the old H200 body**; the Deluxe, Grandia and Super Grandia variants are the **new H300** (2019 on). Different engines, different parts, different maintenance logic — know which one you're pricing.
- **PMS is every 5,000 km or 6 months, whichever comes first** — and at fleet mileage, the kilometres always come first. A van running 5,000 km a month needs a **PMS every month**.
- **The warranty is 3 years *or* 100,000 km, whichever comes first.** At 60,000 km/year you exhaust it in roughly **20 months**. The advertised "5-year warranty" is largely unreachable for a hardworking van.
- **Both engines use a timing chain**, no belt replacement to budget — but both are interference designs, and the 1GD has a documented chain-tensioner rattle worth catching early.
- **The DPF is the biggest city-route risk** on the 2.8. A manual regeneration switch has been standard since February 2019 — learn to use it.
- **Owner sentiment is genuinely strong**: 4.27/5 across 84 PH owner reviews. The complaints are ride harshness and basic trim, not reliability.

## Which Hiace Does This Guide Cover? (Two Generations, Sold Side by Side)

This is the single most important thing to sort out before you budget a peso, and most price lists blur it completely.

Toyota Philippines sells **two different generations of Hiace concurrently**:

**The old H200 body** — the classic upright van shape that's been on sale here since the mid-2000s. It survives at the bottom of the range as the **Cargo** and the **3.0L Commuter**, powered by the **3.0L 1KD-FTV** producing around **136 PS and 300 Nm**, with a **5-speed manual** only. It's still on sale for one reason: price. Fleet and PUV buyers who need seats and cargo volume per peso keep buying it.

**The new H300** — the wider, semi-bonneted van that **world-premiered right here in Manila on February 18, 2019** (a genuine rarity: Toyota launched it in the Philippines before anywhere else, with retail starting March 5, 2019). This is the **Deluxe Cargo, Commuter Deluxe, GL Grandia, GL Grandia Tourer** and **Super Grandia** family, all running the **2.8L 1GD-FTV** — the same engine family as the [Hilux](/blog/toyota-hilux-pms-guide), [Fortuner](/blog/toyota-fortuner-pms-guide) and diesel [Innova](/blog/toyota-innova-pms-guide) — at roughly **163–176 PS and 420–450 Nm**, with 6-speed manual or 6-speed automatic.

<Pullquote>If a quote, a spec sheet, or a used listing says "Hiace" without saying which body, it hasn't told you what you're buying.</Pullquote>

Practically, that means: **a 3.0L Commuter and a 2.8L Commuter Deluxe are not the same van** despite similar names. Different engine, different platform, different safety equipment, and a price gap of several hundred thousand pesos. This guide covers both, and flags where they diverge.

## PMS Interval Chart

Standard Toyota Philippines cadence, **every 5,000 km or 6 months, whichever comes first**:

| Odometer | What's typically done |
|---|---|
| 1,000 km | First check-up — inspection, bolt torque, fluid levels |
| 5,000 km | Engine oil + oil filter, general inspection |
| 10,000 km | Oil + filter, tyre rotation, brake inspection |
| 20,000 km | Oil + filter, air cleaner element, cabin filter |
| 30,000 km | Oil + filter, cabin filter, deeper brake inspection |
| **40,000 km** | **Heavy stop** — oil + filter, fuel filter, brake fluid, air cleaner, coolant check |
| 50,000 km | Oil + filter, rotation, inspections |
| 60,000 km | Oil + filter, air cleaner, cabin filter |
| **80,000 km** | **Heavy stop** — fuel filter, brake fluid, transmission and differential oils, full driveline service |
| 100,000 km+ | Continue the 5,000 km rhythm; coolant, injectors and DPF condition come into focus |

Note the **differential and transmission oils** at the heavy stops — the Hiace is **rear-wheel drive**, so it has a rear diff a front-drive MPV doesn't. Don't let a shop skip it.

## Light vs. Heavy (Major) PMS — and What It Actually Costs

**Light PMS** is the every-5,000 km service: engine oil, oil filter, inspection. Fast, cheap, and the one you'll do most.

**Heavy PMS** lands at **40,000 and 80,000 km**, where fuel filter, brake fluid, air cleaner, and — at 80K — driveline oils all come due at once. This is where the bill jumps, and where operators get caught out if they haven't reserved for it.

## The Number That Actually Matters: Cost Per Kilometre

Here's where a Hiace guide has to depart from a family-car guide. A private owner asks "how much per PMS?" An operator has to ask **"how much per kilometre?"** — because that's what you're pricing your service against.

The arithmetic is simple and worth doing with your own numbers:

**Servicing cost per km = (cost of one light PMS ÷ 5,000 km)**, plus a reserve for the heavy stops.

At an estimated ₱4,500–7,500 per light PMS, that's roughly **₱0.90–1.50 per kilometre on scheduled servicing alone** — before fuel, tyres, brakes, insurance, or the driver. Add the 40K and 80K heavy services amortised across the interval and you're realistically reserving **₱1.20–1.90 per kilometre** for maintenance on a 2.8L H300.

Now the part that surprises people:

<Pullquote>At 5,000 km a month, "every 5,000 km or 6 months" means a PMS every single month — twelve services a year, not two.</Pullquote>

A shuttle van doing 200 km a day, 25 days a month, covers 5,000 km monthly. That's **12 PMS visits a year**, each one a half-day the van isn't earning. Two things follow:

1. **Downtime is a real cost line.** Twelve service visits a year at half a day each is roughly a week of lost operating days. Book the earliest slot, or find a casa that will take the van on a Sunday.
2. **The 6-month leg of the interval is irrelevant to you.** It only binds low-mileage private owners. If you're an operator, your schedule is purely kilometre-driven — set a reminder at every 5,000 km on the odometer and ignore the calendar.

For a private family Super Grandia doing 12,000 km a year, the picture inverts completely: you'll hit the **6-month** limit long before 5,000 km, so you're on **two services a year** and the running cost per km is far less alarming.

## The Warranty Trap Every Fleet Buyer Should Know

This is the single most valuable thing in this guide, and almost nobody spells it out.

Toyota Philippines' manufacturer warranty is **3 years or 100,000 km — whichever comes first.** Vehicles bought from January 1, 2025 can extend to a **5-year** term, but that extension is conditional on completing a minimum number of PMS visits (at least six, with at least one per year) **inside that original 3-year/100,000 km window**.

Read that against real fleet mileage:

| Annual mileage | Time to hit 100,000 km | Warranty reality |
|---|---|---|
| 12,000 km (private family use) | ~8 years | Full 3 years, extension achievable |
| 30,000 km (light commercial) | ~3.3 years | Roughly the full 3 years |
| 60,000 km (active shuttle) | **~20 months** | Warranty ends well inside year 2 |
| 100,000 km (heavy route work) | **~12 months** | Warranty ends in about a year |

**A hardworking Hiace exhausts its warranty on kilometres, not years — and the "5-year warranty" headline is effectively unreachable for it.** That isn't a scandal, it's how the terms are written; it's just that the marketing is aimed at a private buyer and the Hiace's typical buyer is not one.

What to do about it:

- **Budget as if the warranty is 100,000 km**, and treat the 3 years as the irrelevant number.
- **Front-load your casa relationship.** Stay at the dealer through that first 100,000 km — that's when a defect claim is actually live and worth protecting.
- **After the km cap, the calculus opens up completely.** A van at 100,000 km with no coverage left has no warranty reason to stay at the casa, and diesel Toyotas are exactly the kind of vehicle an independent shop handles well. Our [dealer-versus-independent servicing breakdown](/blog/casa-vs-talyer-philippines) covers what to move out and what to keep at the dealer permanently — for a fleet, that decision is worth serious money at twelve services a year.
- **We could not confirm whether commercial or fleet-registered units have separate warranty terms.** Ask the dealer directly and get the answer in writing before you sign.

## Known Issues — What's Actually Documented

Following our standing rule: verified or engine-family-level only, clearly labelled, nothing invented.

**DPF clogging on short city routes (2.8L H300).** The headline maintenance risk. A diesel particulate filter needs sustained heat to burn off soot, and a van doing short hops in traffic never gets there. Toyota fitted a **manual DPF regeneration switch as standard on the new-generation Hiace from February 2019** — which tells you they expected this to matter. Learn where that switch is and what the dashboard warning looks like. The free preventive medicine is a **weekly sustained highway run**. This is the same DPF logic covered in our [Hilux reliability guide](/blog/toyota-hilux-common-problems), including the "oil dilution" side effect where repeatedly aborted regens thin the engine oil.

**Timing chain tensioner wear (1GD family).** Documented at engine-family level, with a technical bulletin covering chain noise on post-May-2017 1GD engines traced to supply pump and crankshaft alignment. The symptom is a **rattle on cold start**. Both Hiace engines are **interference** designs, so this is not a noise to live with. PH-specific prevalence is unconfirmed.

**Injector and fuel-system wear at high mileage.** Both the 1KD and 1GD are common-rail diesels, and injector seat leakage is the classic high-mileage failure mode across the family — on the older 1KD it's a well-known pattern that can damage the turbo if ignored. At fleet mileage you will meet this eventually; budget for it rather than being shocked by it.

**Ride harshness — the actual owner complaint.** Across **84 owner reviews on Zigwheels PH the Hiace rates 4.27/5**, which is high. The recurring criticisms are **stiff, rough ride** (especially unladen or fully loaded), **basic interior**, difficult entry and exit on some configurations, and **fuel consumption on long trips**. Notably absent from owner complaints: engine failures, transmission failures, or any catastrophic defect pattern. The reputation for running past **300,000 km in commercial service** appears well earned.

**On recalls: we found no publicly reported PH recall or service campaign covering the Hiace.** Be careful how you read that — the Philippines has no searchable public recall database, so this is "nothing found," not "nothing exists." Toyota PH's recent campaigns (the 2025 instrument cluster recall, and the December 2025 Parking Assist ECU campaign covering Alphard, Camry, Land Cruiser, Prado and RAV4) do **not** include the Hiace. Check your VIN on Toyota PH's service campaign page — recall work is free, but casa-only.

## Casa vs. Talyer & Parts

**Through the first 100,000 km → casa**, for the reasons above: that's your entire live warranty window, and on a commercial vehicle you want the claim intact.

**After that, the Hiace is an excellent candidate for independent servicing.** The 1GD is one of the most widely deployed diesel engines in the country — shared with the Hilux, Fortuner and Innova, which between them sell tens of thousands of units a year. Every diesel-savvy shop in the Philippines knows this engine, and parts are everywhere at competitive prices. The older 1KD in the Cargo and 3.0L Commuter is even more deeply established, with decades of parts supply behind it.

Two non-negotiables when you go outside:

1. **Low-ash DL-1 diesel oil on the 2.8.** Not "any diesel oil." The low-ash formulation is what protects the DPF, and a shop that shrugs at the distinction is a shop that will cost you a DPF.
2. **DPF forced regeneration needs the proper scan tool.** If the filter is genuinely loaded, a manual switch cycle may not be enough and the procedure has to be commanded properly. That's a casa or a well-equipped specialist, not a roadside talyer.

## Buying Used: What to Check

Given how many Hiaces work for a living, a used one may have lived a much harder life than its age suggests.

- **Establish the mileage honestly.** A 5-year-old shuttle van could be at 300,000 km. Ask for service records — and per the resale logic in our servicing guide, a seller with a complete paper trail is telling you something real.
- **Identify the generation on sight**, not from the ad. Old upright body = H200/3.0L. Wider, semi-bonneted nose = H300/2.8L.
- **Cold-start it yourself.** Listen for chain rattle. Watch for smoke that doesn't clear.
- **Check for a DPF warning history** and ask directly whether it's ever been in limp mode. On a city-route van this is the question.
- **Inspect the rear suspension and the load floor.** Sagging leaves and a beaten floor tell you what the van actually did for a living.
- **Check the VIN against Toyota PH's campaign page** before money changes hands.

## The Bottom Line

The Hiace earns its place as the country's fifth best-selling vehicle by being genuinely hard to kill — the owner data backs that up, and 300,000 km is a normal working life, not an outlier.

But it rewards being run as a **business asset rather than a car**. That means: know which of the two generations you actually own, set your PMS by the odometer and not the calendar, reserve roughly ₱1.20–1.90 per kilometre for maintenance, treat the warranty as a **100,000 km** allowance rather than a three-year one, and take the DPF seriously if your routes are short and urban. Do those five things and the Hiace will out-earn almost anything else with a Toyota badge on it.

For the wider cost picture, our [guide to PMS meaning, schedule and pricing across the market](/blog/car-pms-philippines-guide) puts these numbers in context against the rest of the range — and if you're weighing a diesel Toyota for work, the [Hilux](/blog/toyota-hilux-pms-guide) shares this van's engine family and much of its maintenance logic.
