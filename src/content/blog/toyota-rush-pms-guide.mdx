The **Toyota Rush** is the odd one out in Toyota Philippines' seven-seater lineup, and that's exactly why it still has fans. While the Avanza, Veloz and every rival MPV moved to front-wheel drive with a CVT, the Rush kept a **ladder-frame chassis, rear-wheel drive, 220 mm of ground clearance and a plain 4-speed automatic**. That old-school hardware changes what you actually maintain — there's a rear differential and a propeller shaft on this car that a front-wheel-drive crossover simply doesn't have. So "magkano ba to imaintain?" has a genuinely different answer here. Here's the full picture: the interval chart, the cost by stage, the timing-chain answer, the transmission and rear-diff items nobody mentions, the one real Philippine recall, and what eight years of owner feedback actually says. This is the Rush companion to our [complete guide to PMS meaning, schedule and cost](/blog/car-pms-philippines-guide).

## Key Takeaways

- **PMS schedule:** every **5,000 km or 6 months** — Toyota's standard cycle for its 1.5L gasoline models. Oil + filter each light PMS; heavy/major service around **40,000 km and 80,000 km**.
- A **light/standard PMS** should be about **₱4,500–7,000**; the first PMS is **₱1,500–2,500**. *(Estimates — no Rush-specific casa price list exists. Verify with your dealer, July 2026.)*
- **Timing belt or chain?** **Chain** — no scheduled belt job, ever.
- **It's rear-wheel drive on a ladder frame** — so budget for **rear differential oil and propeller-shaft inspection**, items FWD rivals don't have. The upside: **no CVT**, so no CVT-fluid worry at all.
- **One variant left.** Toyota PH cut the entry-level 1.5 E in **January 2024**; only the **1.5 GR-S A/T (~₱1.2M)** remains.
- **One real PH recall** — the Denso fuel pump, covering **18,484 Rush units built April 2018 to July 2019**. Free. Check your VIN.
- **No Toyota Safety Sense** on any Rush — six airbags and stability control, but no autonomous braking or lane keeping.

## Which Model Year Does This Guide Cover? (2018–2026)

This guide covers the **second-generation Toyota Rush**, launched in the Philippines on **May 5–6, 2018** at an event in Bonifacio Global City, through the current **2026** model year. It's built on Daihatsu's **F800** platform — the same car sold as the third-generation Daihatsu Terios — and is **CBU-imported from Indonesia** rather than locally assembled.

Mechanically, **nothing important has changed in eight years**: same 1.5L 2NR-VE engine, same ladder frame, same rear-wheel drive, same 4-speed automatic. What changed is the lineup, and that's worth knowing whether you're buying new or used:

- **May 2018 (launch).** Three variants: 1.5 E MT ₱948,000, 1.5 E AT ₱988,000, and the seven-seat 1.5 G AT ₱1,070,000.
- **November 2021 ("improved Rush").** All variants became genuine **7-seaters** — the E grade gained the third row — and the reverse camera from the G grade filtered down to the E. Prices moved to E MT ₱983,000 / E AT ₱1,023,000 / G AT ₱1,100,000.
- **May 17, 2022 (facelift).** The G was discontinued and replaced by the **GR-S**, which brought **all-LED headlamps with L-shaped LED daytime running lights**, a GR Sport body kit and 17-inch alloys. Launch price around ₱1,176,000.
- **January 12, 2024 (the big one).** Toyota PH discontinued the **Rush 1.5 E** in both MT and AT as part of a line-up consolidation, on the same day it introduced the **Veloz 1.5 E CVT** at ₱1,092,000 to take over that entry-level slot. From that point the Rush has been a **single-variant model**.
- **Today.** Only the **1.5 GR-S A/T** is sold, at roughly **₱1.2 million** — published SRPs vary a little by source (₱1,196,000 in early 2024, now listed between ₱1,202,000 and ₱1,208,000), so confirm the current figure at the dealer. Three colours: White, Black Metallic and Dark Red Mica Metallic.

**Two honest notes on the model's future.** Toyota has **not announced a next-generation Rush**, and the model was **discontinued in Malaysia** over slow sales. It's still on sale here and still selling — **3,111 units in 2025** — but it's now a low-volume nameplate in Toyota PH's range rather than a core one. That's a parts-and-resale consideration, not a reliability one; the engine and most consumables are shared with Toyota's highest-volume cars, which is the point of the next section.

## Toyota Rush Maintenance Schedule (PMS Interval Chart)

The Rush follows Toyota's standard **every 5,000 km or 6 months** cadence — oil and filter plus a general inspection at every stop, with the bigger-ticket items landing at the 40,000 km and 80,000 km "heavy" services.

| Odometer (km) | PMS type | Key items done |
|---|---|---|
| 1,000 | First check (free) | First inspection, oil level + bolt-torque check, no major parts |
| 5,000 | Light | Engine oil + oil filter, general inspection |
| 10,000 | Light | Oil + filter, tire rotation, brake inspection (**rear drums** cleaned/adjusted) |
| 15,000 | Light | Oil + filter, general inspection |
| 20,000 | Light | Oil + filter, fuel-system check, **propeller shaft + rear axle inspection** |
| 30,000 | Light | Oil + filter, **cabin air filter**, brake + suspension inspection |
| **40,000** | **Heavy / major** | Oil + filter, **brake fluid, engine air cleaner element, ATF service, rear differential oil**, full inspection |
| 50,000–70,000 | Light | Oil + filter each 5k/6-mo stop, rotating inspections |
| **80,000** | **Heavy / major** | Everything at 40K, plus suspension, bushing and body-mount inspection |

*Whichever comes first — km or months. Longer-life items sit further out: **iridium spark plugs at around 100,000 km** and engine coolant well beyond that. The exact km for brake fluid, plugs, ATF and rear diff oil follows Toyota's general pattern rather than a published Rush-specific Philippine sheet, so treat this chart as the shape of the schedule and confirm the specifics in your owner's manual. One thing that is not negotiable: Metro Manila and South Luzon traffic and heat put you squarely in Toyota's own "severe driving conditions" definition — don't stretch the oil interval to save a few hundred pesos.*

## Light PMS vs. Heavy PMS on the Rush

Most Rush visits are **light PMS** — engine oil and filter plus a general check, every 5,000 km. Quick, cheap, nothing exotic. The rear **drum brakes** actually help here: drums are cheaper to service than discs, and a brake job on the rear axle is a modest bill.

The **heavy (major) PMS** arrives around **40,000 km and 80,000 km**, and on the Rush it carries more items than it would on a front-wheel-drive rival. On top of the oil change: brake fluid and the engine air-cleaner element, an **automatic transmission fluid service**, and — the one people forget — **rear differential oil**. Spark plugs come further out, around 100,000 km. That's why the 40K bill roughly doubles a light PMS and the 80K climbs higher still.

Budget for those two stops in advance. Hindi porket matipid ang araw-araw na PMS, wala nang malaking singil paminsan-minsan.

## Toyota Rush: Timing Belt or Chain?

**Chain.** The 1.5L 2NR-VE is a timing-chain design, so there is **no scheduled timing-belt replacement** in the Rush's life — you never face the ₱15,000–30,000+ job that belt-driven cars meet around 100,000 km. A chain is designed to last the life of the engine, *on clean oil*.

That caveat matters more than it sounds. This is an **interference engine**, and the 2NR's dual variable valve timing depends on correct oil pressure and the right viscosity to work properly. A stretched chain, a rattling tensioner or a tired VVT actuator on this engine family is almost always a consequence of skipped or wrong-spec oil changes, not a design fault. **On-time oil, correct grade (0W-20), is the entire maintenance strategy for this engine.**

## No CVT — but You Do Have a Rear Diff: the Driveline Items

This is where the Rush genuinely parts ways from every other seven-seater at its price, and it cuts both ways.

**The good news: there's no CVT.** The Rush uses a conventional **4-speed automatic** with ordinary automatic transmission fluid. No belt-and-pulley assembly to fatigue, no CVT-specific fluid at ₱600–1,000 a litre, no anxiety about whether the previous owner ever changed it. Every talyer in the country understands a torque-converter automatic. Ask the casa for the exact Toyota ATF spec for your unit and the drain interval in your manual — the common practice is a drain-and-fill in the neighbourhood of 40,000 km, but Toyota PH publishes no Rush-specific figure we could verify, so get it from the manual rather than from a forum.

**The item nobody mentions: the rear driveline.** Because the Rush sends power to the rear wheels, it has hardware a front-wheel-drive Avanza, Veloz or Xpander does not:

- **A rear differential**, running on gear oil that needs periodic replacement — a long-interval item, not a frequent one, but it belongs on your checklist. Many owners go the life of the car without touching it because nobody told them it existed.
- **A propeller shaft** with universal joints and a centre bearing. These want inspection, and a vibration or a clunk on take-up usually traces here rather than to the engine or gearbox.
- **A live rear axle on a ladder frame**, which means **body mounts and bushings** are wear items in their own right — the source of the squeaks and rattles long-term owners report.

None of this is expensive. All of it is easy to skip for eight years and then pay for at once. Sabihin mo sa casa na i-check ang rear diff at propeller shaft sa 40,000 km — hindi lahat ng service advisor mag-o-offer nito kung hindi ka magtatanong.

## Known Issues on the Toyota Rush

The Rush has been on sale here since 2018, so unlike the newer models we've covered, there's a real ownership record to draw on — and it's a good one. Philippine owner sentiment on Zigwheels sits at roughly **4.27 out of 5 across 94 owner reviews**. There is **no widespread mechanical-defect pattern**. What exists is one genuine recall and a consistent set of comfort and drivetrain-character complaints.

**The one real Philippine recall: Denso low-pressure fuel pump.** Toyota Motor Philippines' fuel-pump campaign covered more than 30,000 Avanza and Rush units, of which **18,484 were Rush units built between April 2, 2018 and July 4, 2019**. The pump's plastic impeller was made with insufficient molecular density and can crack and deform, causing the pump to fail. Symptoms are warning lights, hard or failed starting, rough running, and stalling at low speed — in rare cases at higher speed. **Replacement is free of charge**, regardless of the car's age or where you bought it. Check your chassis number on Toyota's Service Campaign site or at any dealer.

*Worth stating clearly because it causes confusion: the **February 2024 front shock absorber nut recall did not include the Rush**. That campaign covered the Avanza, Veloz and Yaris Cross, which sit on a completely different front-wheel-drive platform.*

Beyond the recall, these are the recurring owner and reviewer complaints — all character and comfort issues, none of them failures:

- **The 4-speed automatic is the weak link.** With only four ratios and 134 Nm to work with, owners report it **hunting between 3rd and 4th around 80–90 km/h**, and reviewers note you need to wind it to roughly 3,500–4,000 rpm before real pull arrives. It's not a defect — it's an old gearbox with too few gears for expressway work. Take a loaded test drive before you commit.
- **Cabin noise at highway speed.** Consistently the most-mentioned gripe. A ladder-frame body with modest sound deadening is simply louder than a monocoque crossover.
- **Weak rear air conditioning for the third row.** A recurring family complaint, and a genuine consideration if you'll regularly carry seven people in PH heat.
- **Firm ride.** The ladder frame and rear axle give durability and load tolerance; the trade is a busier ride over broken pavement, especially unladen.
- **Light steering that feels vague at speed.** Great for parking and tight streets, less reassuring on the expressway or a winding road.
- **Third row best for kids or short trips.** Owners praise the space overall but are consistent that adults in row three is a short-distance proposition.
- **Squeaks and rattles with age.** Body-on-frame construction develops them; owners report this as a long-term nuisance rather than a fault.
- **Engine issues at very high mileage.** Philippine owner reports place these in the **150,000–200,000 km** range — which is to say, at the mileage where any hard-worked 1.5L needs real attention, not an early-life problem.

On the praise side, the consistent themes are fuel economy, the 220 mm ground clearance (genuinely useful in flood-prone areas), the seven-seat practicality and plain reliability. We'll update this section if a verified mechanical pattern emerges.

## Toyota Rush Warranty — and Why On-Time PMS Pays

Every new Rush comes with Toyota Motor Philippines' standard **3-year / 100,000 km** manufacturer warranty. Since January 2025 Toyota PH has offered a **5-Year Warranty** — the 3-year base plus up to an added **2 years / 40,000 km Service Loyalty Warranty** — *but only if you keep your PMS at a Toyota dealer* and complete the minimum number of services during the warranty period. Sticking to the casa schedule during the warranty years isn't just good hygiene; it's what unlocks the extra coverage. ## Casa vs. Talyer & Parts

While the car is under warranty — and especially if there's outstanding recall work — **the casa is the right answer**. Fuel-pump recall work is free at the dealer and nowhere else.

**Out of warranty**, a trusted talyer usually undercuts the casa on routine work, and the Rush is well set up for it. The **2NR-VE engine and its consumables are shared with the Avanza, Veloz, Vios and Ativ** — some of the highest-volume cars in the Philippines — so oil, filters, plugs and brake parts are cheap and available everywhere, and a conventional 4-speed automatic holds no mysteries for an independent shop. The rear drum brakes are cheaper to service than discs.

Two caveats. First, the body is **CBU-imported from Indonesia**, so model-specific panels, lights and trim carry longer lead times than a locally-assembled Toyota — and with the Rush now a single-variant, low-volume model here, that gap is more likely to widen than narrow. Second, **rear driveline work** (differential, propeller shaft) is genuinely specialist compared with a FWD car — find a shop that's actually done it rather than one improvising.

Between visits, DIY the basics: tire pressure, washer fluid, cabin filter, an oil-level eyeball. Leave the ATF, the rear diff and anything recall-related to the pros.

## Verdict: Old-School Hardware, Genuinely Cheap to Keep

The Rush is a straightforward car to own, and its dated hardware is mostly *in its favour* on the maintenance side. The **2NR-VE is a timing-chain engine shared with Toyota's best-selling cars**, so parts are cheap and universally available. There's **no CVT** to worry about and no belt job on the horizon. The rear drums are inexpensive. The 5,000 km cycle is the plain Toyota one. For flood-prone routes and rough provincial roads, **220 mm of clearance and a ladder frame** are real advantages that a low crossover can't answer.

The honest asterisks are equally clear. The **4-speed automatic is the car's weakest point** and it shows most on the expressway. There's **no Toyota Safety Sense**, and the 5-star ASEAN NCAP result is a **2018 test under an older protocol**. It's now a **single-variant, low-volume model** with no announced successor and CBU-imported body parts. And rear-wheel drive means the **rear differential and propeller shaft are yours to remember** — nobody else will bring them up.

Do that, keep the oil on time and on grade, budget for the 40K and 80K stops, and the Rush is an easy and inexpensive seven-seater to live with. Cross-shopping?
