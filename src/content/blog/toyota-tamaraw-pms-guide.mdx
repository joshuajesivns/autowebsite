Some nameplates sell cars. A few of them end up in the language itself.

The **Toyota Tamaraw** is one of maybe two or three vehicles in Philippine history that managed the second thing. For a whole generation, "FX" stopped meaning a model and started meaning *a way of getting somewhere* — you didn't ride a Tamaraw FX, you rode **an FX**. That's not marketing. That's a vehicle so woven into how a country moved that it became a common noun.

Then in 2005 it disappeared. And in December 2024, after almost two decades, Toyota brought it back — built in Santa Rosa, Laguna, on a platform designed for exactly the customer the original was designed for.

This guide covers both halves: **the full history of the nameplate**, and then everything an owner or buyer needs on the new one — variants, payload, the genuinely unusual PMS pricing, and one warranty detail that catches people out.

## Key Takeaways

- **The nameplate ran from December 1976 to 2005**, spanning four generations, and sold roughly **140,919 units in the FX and Revo eras alone**.
- **The Tamaraw FX became a common noun** — the direct ancestor of today's UV Express. That cultural footprint is the real reason this revival matters.
- **The new one is genuinely locally built** in Santa Rosa, Laguna, on a **₱5.5-billion investment** including a dedicated **₱1.1-billion Conversion Factory**.
- **Toyota published a fixed PMS price — ₱1,200 per visit up to 40,000 km**, then ~₱3,100 (gas) / ~₱3,800 (diesel). Almost no model in this market gets a published, model-specific number like that.
- **The warranty is split:** 3 years/100,000 km on the vehicle, but only **20,000 km or 12 months on conversion bodies.**
- **Payload runs ~830 kg to ~1,125 kg** depending on body, and the diesel is the 2.4L 2GD-FTV — **timing chain**, shared with the Hilux and Fortuner.

## The History of the Toyota Tamaraw (1976–2005)

### First generation (1976–1981): the Basic Utility Vehicle

The Tamaraw arrived in **December 1976**, assembled by **Delta Motors in Bicutan, Parañaque** — Toyota's Philippine assembler at the time. It was conceived as a **Basic Utility Vehicle**: cheap, simple, body-on-frame, leaf-sprung, and built to survive roads that were often not roads at all.

The specification tells you everything about the brief. A **1.2-litre inline-four making around 61 hp**, a four-speed manual, circular headlamps, and a body you could have as a high-side pickup or a **15-seater jeepney-style** people carrier.

The name came from the **tamaraw** — *Bubalus mindorensis*, the dwarf buffalo endemic to Mindoro. A small, stubborn, locally-native beast of burden. Nobody has ever named a vehicle more accurately.

Internationally, the same basic vehicle was sold as the **Toyota Kijang** across Southeast Asia, Taiwan and India. The Tamaraw was the Philippine expression of it.

### Second generation (1981–1991): the Super Diesels

The second generation grew to a **1.3-litre engine at around 71 hp**, keeping the same body choices. This is the generation that became known for its **"Super Diesel"** variants, and the one that simply *was there* through the defining decade — it drove through the **1986 People Power Revolution** and the turbulence around it, hauling goods and people while the country rearranged itself.

### Third generation — the Tamaraw FX (1991–1998): the one that entered the language

This is the important one.

The first FX **rolled off the Philippine line on October 8, 1991** in high-side pickup form. The **wagon** — the shape everyone actually pictures — followed in **December 1992**, and Toyota marketed it hard from **1993** under the tagline **"Wonder Vehicle,"** in a campaign fronted by **Darna**.

Mechanically it was modest: a **1.5-litre gasoline or 2.0-litre diesel**, five-speed manual, roughly 64–78 hp depending on version and year. A 1996 update added **power steering and seatbelts**. Air-conditioning and a radio-cassette made it, by the standards of a Filipino utility vehicle, almost civilised.

But the FX did something no spec sheet predicted. It was **big enough to carry a family, cheap enough for a small business, and tough enough to run all day** — which meant thousands of them ended up in informal point-to-point passenger service. Fixed routes, shared rides, leaving when full.

<Pullquote>The FX didn't just win its segment. It invented a category of Philippine public transport — and then lent that category its own name.</Pullquote>

People stopped saying "I'll take a Tamaraw FX" and started saying **"FX na lang tayo."** The word detached from the vehicle entirely. That informal service is the direct ancestor of what's now regulated as **UV Express**. Baguio in particular ran fleets of them as taxis.

Toyota sold roughly **70,960 third-generation units between 1991 and 1998**.

### Fourth generation — the Tamaraw FX Revo (1998–2004)

The **Tamaraw FX Revo**, later just the **Revo**, softened the boxy shape into something rounder and more SUV-like, while keeping the body-on-frame toughness underneath. Engines moved up to a **1.8-litre gasoline or 2.4-litre diesel**, with the strongest versions around **105 hp and 161 Nm**, and higher trims got front disc brakes, alloy wheels, and even leather.

It was the Tamaraw growing up — still a workhorse, now also a family car people actively wanted rather than settled for. Roughly **69,959 units** were sold between 1999 and 2004.

### The end (2005)

Production stopped in **2005**. Toyota was consolidating globally onto the **IMV** platform — Innovative International Multi-purpose Vehicle — and the Philippine answer to what the Revo had been became the **Innova**.

Combined, the FX and Revo generations accounted for about **140,919 units**. The nameplate went quiet for nearly twenty years.

## The Revival: What the New Tamaraw Actually Is

Toyota Motor Philippines launched the **Next Generation Tamaraw on December 6, 2024**, simultaneously at eight venues nationwide.

It's built on **IMV 0** — Toyota's deliberately back-to-basics take on the IMV idea, a cheap and rugged cab-chassis platform. Its close sibling is the **Toyota Hilux Champ** sold elsewhere in Asia. Where the modern Hilux has grown into a ₱2-million lifestyle vehicle, IMV 0 goes back to what IMV originally meant: a tool.

**And it's genuinely made here.** Not assembled from a kit as an afterthought — Toyota put **₱5.5 billion** into it, including **₱1.1 billion for a new TMP Conversion Factory** in Santa Rosa, Laguna. That 1.5-hectare facility builds the bodies locally: utility van, aluminium van, dropside. There are **33 "Tier 1" local parts suppliers** across Central Luzon, NCR and Southern Tagalog, plus **26 new Tier 2 suppliers**, and a production target of **18,000–22,000 units a year**.

The target customer is stated openly: **MSMEs — micro, small and medium enterprises, about 99% of registered businesses in the country.** Which is, almost word for word, the brief the 1976 original was built to.

And there's a detail that closes the loop nicely: the new Tamaraw has been granted **"Modern PUV" certification by the DOTr** — clearing it for the public-transport role the FX effectively created. Toyota also revived the **"FX" designation itself** for the passenger utility-van body.

## Which Model Years Does This Guide Cover? (2024–2026)

This guide covers the **Next Generation Tamaraw from its December 6, 2024 launch through the 2026 model year**. The vehicle has not been facelifted, but the lineup has moved:

- **Launch pricing has risen materially.** The range opened at **₱750,000** for the 2.0 SWB Dropside MT in December 2024. The current entry point is the **2.4 Dropside at ₱937,000**, and some early gas variants are no longer prominent on price lists.
- **Automatics expanded in December 2025**, when the GL Utility Van and Aluminum Cargo bodies gained automatic versions.
- **The specialised conversions** — Ambulance, Patrol Car, Telco Van, Wing Van, Mobile Store, Food Truck, Ref Van — have rolled out progressively rather than all at launch.

If you're comparing a 2024 unit against a 2026 one, the mechanicals are the same; what's changed is price, transmission availability and body choice.

**Anything badged "Tamaraw" from 2005 or earlier is a completely different vehicle** — the FX or Revo described above, now a used-market proposition with none of this guide's servicing figures applying to it.

## PMS Interval Chart

Standard Toyota Philippines cadence — **every 5,000 km or 6 months, whichever comes first**:

| Odometer | What's typically done |
|---|---|
| 1,000 km | First check-up — inspection, bolt torque, fluid levels |
| 5,000 km | Engine oil + filter, general inspection |
| 10,000 km | Oil + filter, tyre rotation, brake inspection |
| 20,000 km | Oil + filter, air cleaner, cabin filter |
| 30,000 km | Oil + filter, brake inspection |
| **40,000 km** | **Heavy stop** — fuel filter, brake fluid, air cleaner, coolant check |
| 50,000–70,000 km | Continue the 5,000 km rhythm |
| **80,000 km** | **Heavy stop** — fuel filter, brake fluid, transmission and differential oils |

The Tamaraw is **rear-wheel drive with a live rear axle**, so differential oil is a real service item at the heavy stops. Don't let a shop skip it — and if the truck works hard and loaded, check it more often than the book says.

## Light vs. Heavy PMS — and the Pricing That Makes This Truck Unusual

Here's where the Tamaraw does something almost no other vehicle in this market does: **Toyota published actual, model-specific PMS pricing.**

<Split image={img1} side="right">
**A fixed ₱1,200 per PMS visit, all the way to the 40,000 km check-up.** After 40,000 km, Toyota quotes an average of about **₱3,100 for the gas** Tamaraw and **₱3,800 for the diesel**.

Put that beside the rest of the market. A light PMS on an ordinary sedan typically runs ₱3,500–7,000, and a diesel workhorse usually costs *more* than a car, not less. The Tamaraw inverts that.

Toyota also claims **PMS parts cost "72 percent less compared to other OEM brands,"** producing up to **66% savings versus another cab-over model**. Treat that as a **manufacturer marketing claim** — it's their comparison, not an independent test, and the rival isn't named. The published ₱1,200 figure is the part you can actually hold them to.

Two honest caveats:

1. These figures come from the **launch-period announcement**. Confirm current rates with your dealer before building a business case on them.
2. **Low PMS pricing is not the same as low running cost.** Tyres, brakes, clutches and suspension on a loaded commercial vehicle wear at a rate no service package covers, and a truck that works hard will meet those bills early.

Still — for a fleet buyer, a **fixed and publicly stated** service price up to 40,000 km removes a genuine planning risk. Most operators have to guess. Tamaraw buyers don't.

## The Warranty Split Nobody Mentions

This deserves its own section, because it's the detail most likely to cost someone money.

- **The vehicle:** 3 years or 100,000 km, whichever comes first.
- **The conversion body:** **20,000 km or 12 months.**

If you buy an Aluminum Van, Ref Van, Wing Van, Food Truck or any of the specialised bodies, **you're buying two things with two different warranties** — the Toyota underneath, and the locally-built body on top. The body's coverage expires dramatically sooner.

That's not unreasonable for a bespoke conversion, and it's not hidden. But it means:

- **Inspect the body hard inside that first year.** Door seals, hinges, latches, insulation, refrigeration if fitted, and any water ingress. Twelve months goes fast, and 20,000 km goes faster on a working vehicle.
- **Ask what "body warranty" actually covers** before signing — structure, paint, fittings, and installed equipment may be treated differently.
- **On the vehicle side, the same km-versus-years logic** we covered in the [Hiace fleet guide](/blog/toyota-hiace-pms-guide) applies: at commercial mileage, 100,000 km arrives long before three years do.

## Known Issues — Too New to Say, and We Won't Pretend Otherwise

The Tamaraw launched in **December 2024**. As of now that's roughly 20 months of real-world use, and the public owner-review pool is thin — **about nine owner reviews on Zigwheels PH**, all positive. That is nowhere near enough to establish a defect pattern, and **we're not going to invent one to fill this section.**

What can be said honestly:

- **The engine is not new.** The 2.4L **2GD-FTV** has years of service in the Hilux and Fortuner, and it's a known, well-understood, timing-chain diesel with parts and expertise everywhere in the country. That's a meaningful de-risking. The reliability questions attached to the GD family — chain tensioner rattle on cold start, injector wear at high mileage — are engine-family matters covered in our [Hilux reliability write-up](/blog/toyota-hilux-common-problems), not Tamaraw-specific findings.
- **The platform is deliberately simple.** Body-on-frame, leaf-sprung, rear-wheel drive, minimal electronics on lower grades. Fewer systems is fewer failure modes.
- **The bodies are the genuine unknown.** The conversions are locally built, and they're the newest part of the whole package. That — not the drivetrain — is where early problems would most plausibly show up, and it's exactly where the warranty is shortest. Worth watching.
- **No PH recall found**, and the Tamaraw wasn't included in Toyota PH's recent campaigns. As always: "none found" isn't "none exists," since the Philippines has no searchable public recall database. Check your VIN.

We'll revisit this section once there's a real body of owner data. That's a promise we'd rather keep than fill with guesses.

## Casa vs. Talyer & Parts

**Stay at the casa through the warranty**, and on this truck that's an easy call for once — at ₱1,200 a visit, the usual "casa is expensive" argument barely applies. There's no money to save by going outside during the covered period.

**After that, the Tamaraw should be one of the cheapest vehicles in the country to run independently.** The 2GD-FTV is shared with the Hilux and Fortuner, two of the best-selling vehicles in the Philippines, so every diesel-capable shop knows it and parts are everywhere. The chassis is deliberately old-fashioned — leaf springs, live axle, no complex electronics to speak of on the working variants — which is exactly the kind of vehicle an independent shop handles well.

For how to make that switch without losing resale value, our [dealer-versus-independent servicing guide](/blog/casa-vs-talyer-philippines) covers the job-by-job breakdown. One Tamaraw-specific note: **conversion-body work is its own trade.** A general talyer that's excellent with a diesel engine may have no business touching a refrigerated box or a wing-van mechanism.

## Choosing the Right Body

Since almost every Tamaraw buyer is buying a tool, pick by the job:

- **Dropside** — the highest payload (~1,125 kg), the cheapest entry (₱937,000 diesel), and the most flexible. If you're not sure, this is the default.
- **Aluminum Cargo** — enclosed and secure, for goods that can't ride in the open.
- **Utility Van / FX** — the passenger body, up to 14 seats on the LWB. This is the one with the Modern PUV certification and the historical bloodline.
- **Specialised conversions** — Ambulance, Ref Van, Food Truck, Mobile Store, Wing Van, Telco, Patrol. Buying these from the factory is usually better than commissioning a third-party build: you get a dealer network, and at least *some* body warranty.
- **Gas vs. diesel** — the 2.0L gas is cheaper to buy and fine for lighter, urban work. For anything genuinely loaded or long-distance, the 2.4L diesel's **343–400 Nm** is the right tool, and diesel's economy advantage compounds over a working life.
- **MT vs. AT** — the manual is cheaper and dominant across the range. The automatic is worth it for stop-go city delivery work where driver fatigue is real.

## The Bottom Line

The Tamaraw is back doing the job it invented for itself: cheap, tough, locally built transport for people who need a vehicle to earn rather than impress. The revival is unusually faithful — same brief, same customer, same factory country, even the FX name restored.

For an owner, the case is strong and unusually transparent: **₱937,000 to start, a published ₱1,200 PMS up to 40,000 km, a 3-year vehicle warranty, and an engine shared with the two best-selling diesels in the country.** The things to actually watch are the **short 20,000 km/12-month conversion-body warranty**, the **unconfirmed DPF question**, and the fact that it's still **too new for a real reliability record**.

But there's something worth saying that isn't on any spec sheet. The 1976 original put a whole generation of Filipino families and small businesses on wheels. The FX went further and gave the country a word for how it travels. Very few vehicles anywhere earn that. **The fact that it's building again in Santa Rosa, for the same kind of buyer, is a genuinely good story** — and this time you can check the maintenance costs before you fall for it.
