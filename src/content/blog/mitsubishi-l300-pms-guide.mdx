There's a particular kind of vehicle that stops being a product and becomes infrastructure. The **Mitsubishi L300** is one of them.

It hauls the *tinda* to the market at four in the morning. It moves the sound system for the fiesta. It's the school service, the water delivery, the hardware store's only truck, the family's first real business asset. It has been doing this in the Philippines since the 1980s, and when Euro 4 emissions rules forced Mitsubishi to stop building it at the start of 2018, enough of the country noticed that **Mitsubishi brought it back** rather than let it die.

The truck that came back is not the one that left. And if you're maintaining the current L300 using habits you learned on the old one, **you are following the wrong schedule** — starting with the single most commonly missed detail on this vehicle.

## Key Takeaways

- **The interval is every 5,000 km or 4 months** — not 6 months. This comes from Mitsubishi Motors Philippines' own published PMS checksheet, and the 4-month calendar leg catches out low-mileage owners constantly.
- **Fully synthetic oil officially doubles the oil interval to 10,000 km / 8 months.** That's not a shop upsell — it's stated on the manufacturer's sheet, and it's the single biggest lever on your cost of ownership.
- **The 4N14 uses a timing chain.** No scheduled belt replacement, unlike the old 4D56, which needed a timing belt *and* balance shaft belts.
- **Three verified PH recalls/campaigns:** a 2024 transmission recall (411 units, possibly shipped with no transmission oil), plus 2020 fuel-hose and seat-strap campaigns.
- **MMPC publishes the schedule but not the prices.** No Philippine manufacturer publishes a per-model PMS price list — so we're building one from owner receipts instead.
- **You're buying a cab and chassis, not a finished vehicle.** The body is a separate purchase from a separate builder, with separate warranty.

## Which Model Years Does This Guide Cover? (2019–2026)

This matters more on the L300 than on almost any vehicle we cover, because **there are two completely different trucks wearing the same badge.**

**This guide covers the current 4N14 Euro 4 truck** — the one Mitsubishi began assembling at its Santa Rosa, Laguna plant in the **third week of September 2019** (at a stated rate of 54 units per day) and has sold, essentially unchanged, through the 2026 model year. If your L300 is a 2020 or later, this is you.

**It does not cover the pre-2018 truck.** That one ran the **4D56**, a 2.5-litre SOHC turbo-diesel with a **timing belt** and balance shaft belts. Mitsubishi ceased production at the start of 2018 when Euro 4 came into force, announced the revival in **April 2019**, and returned with a new engine that makes roughly **40% more power and torque** and about **10% better fuel efficiency** than the outgoing motor.

If you own the old one, most of the chassis advice below still applies — but **the engine sections do not.** Different displacement, different combustion, different timing drive, different service items.

## The 4-Month Rule That Catches L300 Owners Out

**Every 5,000 km or every 4 months, whichever comes first.**

Read that again, because most of the Philippine market runs on a 6-month calendar leg. Toyota, Honda and Nissan all use 6 months on their common models. **The L300's sheet says 4.**

<Pullquote>
Kahit 2,000 km pa lang ang takbo mo, due ka pa rin sa ika-apat na buwan.
</Pullquote>

Why this matters so much on this particular vehicle: **a huge number of L300s are low-mileage, high-duty**. The sari-sari store truck that does 15 km a day. The school service that runs two short routes. The barangay's utility vehicle that mostly sits. Those trucks take **a year and a half** to reach 5,000 km — but they hit the 4-month mark three times in that same period.

If you've been servicing "when the odometer says so," **you have been running long on oil for years.** And short-trip, low-mileage diesel duty is the hardest possible life for engine oil — the engine rarely gets fully hot, moisture and fuel don't boil off, and the oil degrades on the calendar rather than on distance. Which is precisely why the calendar leg exists.

Ang totoo niyan: **hindi mileage ang kalaban ng maraming L300, kundi panahon.**

Two honest caveats before you switch:

1. **The 4-month rule still governs everything else.** The other 5,000 km items — battery check, belt and turbo inspection, driveshaft boots, exhaust, radiator cap — are still on the 5,000 km / 4-month line regardless of your oil. Synthetic stretches the *oil*, not the whole service.

## Light vs. Heavy (Major) PMS

The L300 doesn't use the word "heavy PMS" on its sheet, but the pattern is unmistakable once you see the intervals stacked up.

**Light PMS (5,000 km / 4 months)** — the routine visit:
- Engine oil and oil filter
- **Radiator cap replacement** (yes, at *every* interval — an unusual one, but it's on the sheet)
- Battery fluid level and health via Midtronics, inspected out of the vehicle
- Drive belt condition
- Vacuum pump, intake air hose, **turbocharger** and radiator hose/clamp inspection
- Driveshaft boots, exhaust pipe connections, oil-leak check on engine/transmission/differential
- Tyres, lights, horn, aircon, brake and clutch pedal freeplay
- AC cabin filter clean

**Heavy / major stops** — where the bills land:
- **15,000 km / 12 months:** diesel fuel filter, valve clearance check, **SIQL**, air cleaner element, cabin filter replacement, brake pad inspection, tyre rotation, wheel alignment, emission test
- **30,000 km / 24 months:** **gear oil for the manual transmission, transfer and rear differential**, PCV and EGR system, blow-by gas device and catalytic converter, brake shoes and drums, wheel bearings, brake caliper overhaul, brake master cylinder and hose
- **45,000 km / 36 months:** **coolant** — the sheet specifies this interval for 4N1-family engines, which includes the 4N14 (cast-iron engines get 30,000 km / 24 months instead)

Because the L300 is **rear-wheel drive with a leaf-sprung live axle**, the differential and manual gearbox oils at 30,000 km are real, non-skippable service items — not the afterthought they'd be on a front-drive car. On a truck that carries weight, they matter more, not less.

## Timing Belt or Chain?

**Chain.** The 4N14 is chain-driven, and this is one of the genuine upgrades over the truck it replaced.

The official checksheet makes this explicit in a slightly sideways way: it *does* carry a timing belt row — "Replace timing belt including timing belt B and water pump assy, every 70,000 km or 56 months" — but that row is qualified **"except for models with timing chain."** On a current L300, it doesn't apply.

Compare that with the old **4D56**, which needed a timing belt *and* a balance shaft belt on a recurring schedule. Every 70,000-odd kilometres, that was a real, planned, non-trivial bill. **On the current truck, that bill is gone.**

The caveat, and it's the standard one for chain engines: the 4N14 is an **interference engine**. A chain is designed to last the life of the engine, but it lasts that long *on clean oil*. Skip oil changes and you're gambling with a chain that, if it jumps or fails, lets pistons meet valves. **Kung may kalampag sa harap ng makina, ipatingin agad** — don't wait for the next PMS.

## SIQL, EGR, and the Common-Rail Items Talyers Skip

The L300's engine is **common-rail**, and that brings a short list of service items that simply didn't exist on older diesels. These are the ones most likely to be quietly skipped.

**SIQL — Small Injection Quantity Learning** — every 15,000 km / 12 months. The ECU relearns how much fuel each injector actually delivers at very small quantities, then compensates. Injectors drift apart from each other as they age; left unlearned, that shows up as **rough idle, engine noise, and worse fuel consumption**. It's a software procedure — no parts — but it needs the right diagnostic tool, which is exactly why a general talyer may not do it. **Ask specifically whether it was performed.**

**Emission test and DC motor sound check** — every 15,000 km / 12 months, with **EGR cleaning as an additional job based on the inspection result.** Note the phrasing on the sheet: EGR cleaning isn't automatically scheduled, it's triggered by what the inspection finds. For a truck doing short, slow, loaded urban runs — which is most L300s — expect it to be triggered more often than a highway vehicle would.

**PCV and EGR system, blow-by gas reduction device, catalytic converter** — inspection every 30,000 km / 24 months.

**Valve clearance check** — every 15,000 km / 12 months, by sound check, except on engines with auto-lash adjusters. If it needs opening up, the rocker cover gasket gets changed.

## Does the L300 Have a DPF?

**We can't confirm this, and we're not going to guess.**

Here's what we can tell you, precisely. **Mitsubishi's official L300 PMS checksheet contains no diesel particulate filter item anywhere.** It has rows for the EGR system, the PCV system, the blow-by gas reduction device, and the catalytic converter — but no DPF, no regeneration procedure, no ash-service interval. On a document this granular, that absence is meaningful. If the truck had a DPF, you would expect the maintenance sheet to say so.

What you should **not** rely on is the other thing you'll find online. Search the 4N14 and you'll hit real, well-documented DPF clogging problems — including Mitsubishi data on early units needing forced regeneration before 100,000 km. **Those reports are about the Euro 5 European 4N14** in the Outlander and ASX. The L300's 4N14 is a **Euro 4** engine in a different market, in a different state of tune, in a different vehicle. Emissions hardware is exactly the thing that changes between those specs.

So: strong indication there's no DPF, no explicit confirmation from Mitsubishi, and a pile of internet results that are about a different version of the engine. **Ask the dealer directly before you buy**, especially if your routes are short and urban — because if there *were* a DPF, short-trip duty would be the worst thing you could do to it.

## Known Issues and Recalls

Per our house rule, this section contains only things we could actually verify. We're not going to pad it out with invented complaints.

### Verified recalls and campaigns

**1. Transmission recall — announced 3 October 2024.** MMPC recalled **411 units** of the **2024 model year** L300, acting on a directive from Mitsubishi Motors Corporation in Japan. The defect: **the transmission may have left the factory without oil in it**, which produces hard gear shifting. At the time of announcement, **one unit had actually failed.** The remedy is free — if the transmission has no oil, Mitsubishi replaces the **transmission, propeller shaft and snap pin** (about four hours of labour); if it does have oil, they tighten the oil inspection cap with a filler plug and a new gasket.

This is worth taking seriously if you own a 2024 unit. A dry manual gearbox does not announce itself politely.

**2. Fuel hose inspection — 2020 model year.** Where the clearance between the **rubber fuel hose and the alternator pulley** is **less than 10 mm**, the hose can contact the pulley, chafe, and tear — leading to a **fuel leak and possible engine stall or no-start**. MMPC has since applied a production fix for later units. Owners of 2020 units are advised to coordinate with their dealer for inspection.

**3. Seat strap campaign — 2020 model year.** A campaign to prevent possible malfunction of the seat strap. Replacement took about 15 minutes, free of charge.

### On reliability

The owner record here is genuinely, unusually good, and it's worth saying plainly rather than manufacturing balance. Members of the L300 enthusiast and owners' clubs interviewed by Philippine motoring press describe years of delivery, shuttle and family duty without breakdowns — *"ilang beses na siya nasubukan pero never bumigay"* — including sustained use on rough provincial roads and river crossings. The truck's reputation for **cheap maintenance and good parts availability** is real, helped historically by component overlap with the Mitsubishi Adventure.

Isolated owner complaints we found on the current truck were **minor and electrical/fitment** in nature — misaligned headlights, a horn that stopped working, an AC relay cycling. Those are single-owner reports, not a documented pattern, and we're flagging them as exactly that.

**What we deliberately are not claiming:** we found no verified widespread mechanical defect on the current 4N14 L300 beyond the recalls above. That's not the same as saying none exists — the Philippines has no searchable public defect database — but we won't invent one to fill a section.

## Cab and Chassis: You're Buying Two Things

This trips up first-time buyers constantly, so it's worth being blunt about.

**What Mitsubishi sells you is a cab and chassis** — cab, engine, frame, running gear. **There is no body on the back.** The FB body, the dropside, the aluminum van, the multi-purpose van: those are built by **third-party body builders**, and you commission them separately.

Which means:

- **Two purchases, two suppliers, two warranties.** Mitsubishi warrants the truck. The body builder warrants the body. These are not the same document and not the same duration — get both in writing before you pay.
- **Your final price is not the SRP.** ₱829,000 gets you a rolling chassis. Budget the body on top. Listings quoting **₱998,000 for a 17-seater FB** are quoting a finished vehicle, which is why the numbers you see online vary so widely.
- **Body build quality varies enormously.** This is the single biggest variable in whether you end up happy. The Mitsubishi part is a known quantity; the body is whoever you hired.
- **Payload is shared.** The truck's ~1,215 kg payload figure is what the chassis can carry *including the body you bolt to it*. A heavy aluminum van body eats into your cargo allowance before you've loaded anything.

If you're weighing this against a factory-bodied alternative, our [Toyota Tamaraw guide](/blog/toyota-tamaraw-pms-guide) covers a direct rival that takes the opposite approach — Toyota builds the conversion bodies in-house, with a published (if short) body warranty. And for larger passenger duty, the [Hiace ownership breakdown](/blog/toyota-hiace-pms-guide) is the other end of the same market.

## Casa vs. Talyer and Parts

**Stay at the casa through the warranty period**, particularly given the 2024 transmission recall — you want the service history clean and documented if anything driveline-related comes up.

After that, the L300 is close to the ideal candidate for independent servicing, for reasons that are structural rather than sentimental:

- **Every talyer in the country knows this truck.** Decades of installed base means the diagnostic knowledge is genuinely everywhere, not concentrated in dealer networks.
- **Parts are cheap and ubiquitous**, with a deep aftermarket.
- **The chassis is deliberately simple** — leaf springs, live rear axle, RWD, column-shift manual, minimal electronics. This is the kind of vehicle an independent shop handles well.

**The one real exception is the common-rail side.** SIQL needs a diagnostic tool, injector work on a common-rail diesel is precision work, and EGR diagnosis benefits from proper equipment. A shop that's excellent with leaf springs and brakes may be the wrong choice for the fuel system. **Split the work by what it actually requires** — that's not disloyalty to your suko, it's just matching the job to the tools.

## The Bottom Line

The current L300 is a better truck than the one it replaced in the ways that matter to someone running a business: **more power and torque, better economy, and a timing chain instead of a belt** — which quietly removes a recurring five-figure bill from the ownership plan.

What you actually need to hold on to from all of this:

1. **It's 4 months, not 6.** If you take one thing away, take that. Low-mileage L300s are being under-serviced across the country because their owners are counting kilometres.
2. **Fully synthetic officially doubles your oil interval** to 10,000 km / 8 months. Mitsubishi says so on their own sheet.
3. **Check your VIN** if you have a 2024 unit (transmission) or a 2020 unit (fuel hose, seat strap).
4. **Don't skip SIQL** at 15,000 km, and ask by name — it's the item most likely to be quietly omitted.
5. **Budget for the body separately**, and read both warranties.

There's a reason this nameplate outlived its own discontinuation. The L300 was never the most refined thing on the road, and it isn't now. What it is, is a vehicle that a Filipino small business can buy, load, run hard, fix cheaply anywhere, and depend on — **at binibili mo siya hindi para maganda ang tingin, kundi para kumita.** Forty years of that is not an accident.
