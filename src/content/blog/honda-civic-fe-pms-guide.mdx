When Honda Cars Philippines launched the **11th-generation Civic** on **23 November 2021**, it did something it had never done here before: it deleted the naturally-aspirated engine. Every variant — bottom to top — got the **1.5-litre VTEC Turbo.** HCPI's own launch material called it an *"all-turbo variant range,"* and that phrase is the single most useful thing to know about the FE if you are coming from a [10th-generation FC](/blog/honda-civic-fc-pms-guide), where the 1.8 E and 1.8 S were the cars most people actually bought.

There is a second thing to know, and it is less comfortable. **The FE is the most recalled Honda nameplate in this market.** Two separate steering campaigns name it, and the larger one accounts for **2,463 units** of 2022–2024 Civics on its own. Both are free to fix, and neither is a reason to avoid the car — but they are a reason to run your VIN before you do anything else.

This guide covers what the FE costs to keep, what its three variants actually differ on, and the recall picture in full — including a breakdown that resolves a question we had to leave open on two earlier posts. It is the Civic instalment of our [full explainer on PMS meaning, intervals and pricing](/blog/car-pms-philippines-guide).

## Key Takeaways

- **Every FE is turbocharged.** 1.5L DOHC VTEC Turbo, **178 PS / 240 Nm**, across the range — Honda's own figure is **+5 PS and +20 Nm** over the FC's turbo. No cheap NA entry variant exists on this generation.
- **PMS is every 10,000 km or 6 months** — unchanged from the FC, and the Honda brand-wide standard we have now verified across seven models.
- **The V and RS Turbo have a real CVT needing Honda HCF-2. The RS e:HEV's "E-CVT" is not a CVT** — no belt, no pulleys.
- **Timing chain on both engines.** No belt job, ever.
- **Two steering recalls name the Civic.** March 2024: **1,025 units** of the 2022 Civic. January 2025: **2,463 units** of the 2022–2024 Civic 4D, within a 5,505-unit campaign. Free, but check your VIN.
- **The RS e:HEV recorded 30.12 km/L** at a DOE fuel-economy run — on a **155 km, mostly-TPLEX** route. Treat it as a ceiling, not a commute figure.
- **5 stars at ASEAN NCAP, 83.47 overall**, tested October 2021 — but on a **Thailand-market EL+**, and on pillar denominators that **do not** match our HR-V and CR-V figures.
- **Honda Secure Plus does not cover the Civic.** Warranty is **3 years / 100,000 km.** The five-year deal is Prelude and 2026 CR-V only.
- **The S Turbo is gone**, and so is the ₱1,290,000 entry point. The range now starts at **₱1,600,000.**
- **Honda's PM Cost Calculator is still offline** — re-checked **7 September 2026.**

## Which Model Years Does This Guide Cover?

The **11th-generation Civic, chassis code FE**, sold in the Philippines from **23 November 2021** to the present. It replaced the FC, which our [separate FC guide](/blog/honda-civic-fc-pms-guide) covers across its full 2016–2021 span.

**At launch (November 2021)** the range was three all-turbo variants:

| Launch variant | Launch price |
|---|---|
| 1.5 **S** Turbo CVT Honda SENSING | **₱1,290,000** |
| 1.5 **V** Turbo CVT Honda SENSING | **₱1,498,000** |
| 1.5 **RS** Turbo CVT Honda SENSING | **₱1,690,000** |

**The 2024 update changed the shape of the lineup**, not just the styling. The **S was dropped**, the **RS e:HEV hybrid was added** at the **2024 Philippine International Motor Show**, and the turbo cars got a restyled grille with hexagonal inserts, matte-black 18-inch alloys, and red dashboard accents with leather-and-suede seats on the top trim.

Here is where it stands now:

| Variant | Powertrain | Transmission | Price |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1.5 V Turbo CVT** Honda SENSING | 1.5L VTEC Turbo, 178 PS / 240 Nm | CVT | **₱1,600,000** |
| **1.5 RS Turbo CVT** Honda SENSING | 1.5L VTEC Turbo, 178 PS / 240 Nm | CVT | **₱1,830,000** |
| **2.0 RS e:HEV E-CVT** Honda SENSING | 2.0L Atkinson + two motors, **184 PS / 315 Nm** system | E-CVT | **₱1,990,000** |

**Two honest notes on that table.**

First, **Platinum White Pearl costs ₱20,000 more on every single variant** — ₱1,620,000, ₱1,850,000 and ₱2,010,000 respectively. It is a paint charge, not a trim, and it is the reason you will see the Civic's "top price" quoted as both ₱1,990,000 and ₱2,010,000 depending on which listing you read.

Second, **the sources disagree on the RS Turbo.** We found it at **₱1,830,000**, **₱1,790,000** and **₱1,775,000** across aggregators and dealer pages. Prices on this car have moved more than once since the 2024 update. **Confirm at the dealer**; treat every number here as a starting reference.

**One consequence for used buyers.** The **S Turbo only existed from 2021 to the 2024 update**, and it was the cheapest way into a turbocharged Civic at ₱1,290,000. If you are shopping second-hand and see an S, that is what you are looking at — a variant Honda no longer sells, with the same engine and gearbox as the V above it and less equipment.

## The Service Schedule: Every 10,000 km or 6 Months

**Unchanged from the FC, and unchanged by the turbo.** Honda Cars Philippines runs a **10,000 km / 6-month** PMS cadence across its range, and we have now verified it independently on the City, Brio, Jazz, BR-V, HR-V and CR-V.

This is worth stating plainly because it is the most common worry about buying a turbocharged daily: **there is no shortened service interval for the turbo.** The FC's 1.5 RS Turbo ran the same 10,000 km cycle as its 1.8 NA sibling, and the FE runs it on every variant.

Against the compact-sedan competition, most of which sits on a **5,000 km** light-PMS cycle:

| Annual distance | Civic FE visits/yr | A 5,000 km rival's visits/yr |
|---|---|---|
| 10,000 km | 1 | 2 |
| 20,000 km | 2 | 4 |
| To 100,000 km | **10 oil changes** | **20 oil changes** |

<Pullquote>A turbocharged engine on a 10,000 km interval is a genuinely unusual combination in this market. It is also the FE's strongest ownership argument.</Pullquote>

The light service is oil and filter with an inspection round. The heavier work — air cleaner element, cabin filter, brake fluid, spark plugs, and the CVT fluid on the turbo variants — clusters at **40,000 km and 80,000 km.**

**What we will not print: oil grade and capacity.** Honda PH publishes no per-model figure for the FE, and the L15CA is a different engine code from the FC's L15B7 — so carrying the FC guide's numbers across would be a guess dressed up as continuity. This is the **seventh** guide on this site where we have had to leave this row blank rather than repeat a generic database figure. **Your owner's manual is the authority**, and on a direct-injected turbo it matters more than on an NA engine, not less.

## The RS e:HEV: What You're Actually Buying

The hybrid arrived at **PIMS 2024** and is the most powerful Civic variant sold here outside the Type R: **184 PS and 315 Nm** of total system output, from a **2.0-litre Atkinson-cycle** engine and two motors. The powertrain is derived from the larger CR-V e:HEV's, with a **smaller 1.05 kWh** lithium-ion pack against the CR-V's 1.4 kWh.

**The "E-CVT" is not a CVT**, and this is the third post on this site where we have had to say so. Honda's e:HEV is an **i-MMD**-type system: a traction motor, a generator motor, and an engine that spends most of its life generating rather than driving, through a **fixed-gear transaxle with a lock-up clutch** for high-speed cruising. **No belt. No pulleys.** What that means for you:

- **No CVT fluid to worry about**, and no CVT belt to wear. The single most common long-term anxiety about the turbo variants simply does not exist here.
- **Brakes last much longer**, because regenerative braking does most of the slowing.
- **New inspection items instead**: the hybrid battery, its cooling, and the high-voltage system. Casa work, not talyer work.
- **The oil still ages on the calendar.** The engine runs less of the time; the six-month leg of the interval still applies.

### About that 30 km/L

The number is real and it is worth understanding precisely. At a **Department of Energy fuel-economy run in September 2025**, the Civic RS e:HEV recorded **30.12 km/L**, beating the [HR-V](/blog/honda-hr-v-pms-guide) RS e:HEV's **28.08 km/L**. The run covered **155 km, mainly along TPLEX**, with around **70 vehicles** participating across petrol, hybrid and electric.

<Pullquote>A 155-kilometre expressway run is the best-case ceiling for a hybrid, not a description of your commute.</Pullquote>

Read it for what it is: **a controlled, highway-biased, economy-driven route.** Earlier runs put the same car at **24.2 km/L**, and independent road-test figures for the car land nearer the low-20s. All of those are excellent for a 184 PS compact sedan. None of them is what you will see in Manila traffic, where a hybrid's advantage is real but its headline number is not.

## Transmissions and the HCF-2 Rule

**On the V and RS Turbo**, you have a conventional belt-and-pulley **CVT** — the same principle as the City and Jazz — with one non-negotiable requirement: **Honda HCF-2 fluid.** Honda specifically excludes damage caused by non-equivalent fluid from warranty coverage. A shop that pours in generic ATF because *"CVT fluid din naman"* is setting up a failure that costs more than every service you have ever paid for.

The same gap we have hit on the Jazz, BR-V, HR-V and CR-V applies here: **no Honda PH document we could find states a CVT-fluid replacement interval in kilometres for the Civic FE.** Our convention across this site is to treat the **40,000 km heavy service** as the point to do it — and to say openly that this is a convention, not a published spec. **Get your dealer to write the interval into your service booklet.**

On CVT durability specifically, the honest position: the reports we could find of **low-speed CVT hesitation on early 2022–2023 1.5T cars** come from **US owner forums and US service data**, and the same sources note Honda redesigned and recalibrated this generation's CVT, with **no US federal recall or investigation** opened on the CVT assembly. The clearest signal in that data is unglamorous and useful: **owners who actually change the fluid rarely have catastrophic failures.** We found no Philippine pattern for it, and we are reporting that as "we did not find one."

## Timing Belt or Chain?

**Chain, on both engines.** The 1.5 L15CA turbo and the 2.0 Atkinson hybrid unit are both chain-driven, as was every engine in the FC. There is **no scheduled timing-belt replacement** on this car and no ₱15,000–30,000 job waiting around 100,000 km.

What a chain asks for is clean oil on schedule. On a direct-injected turbo, that is not a slogan — it is the maintenance plan.

## Recalls: The FE's Real Story

This is the section that matters most on this car, and it is also where we can now close a gap two of our own earlier posts had to leave open.

### Recall one — March 2024, 1,025 units

HCPI announced a voluntary safety recall of **1,025 units of the 2022 Honda Civic** to replace the **Electric Power Steering gearbox.** In HCPI's own words, the worm gear *"was improperly installed or manufactured. The grease applied on the worm gear was depleted, causing a stick slip leading to an unusual sound when turning the steering wheel."*

The replacement takes **3.5 to 4 hours** and is **free of charge.** HCPI stated that **no related accidents had been reported** in the Philippines.

### Recall two — January 2025, 5,505 units, and the Civic is the largest share

HCPI notified the **Department of Trade and Industry in letters dated 9 January 2025** of two campaigns. The steering one covers **5,505 units** for the same underlying mechanism — grease in the worm gear meshing area decreases with use, sliding resistance and torque fluctuation rise, and the result is unusual noise and "sticky steering" while turning.

**HCPI's own recall pages do not publish a model breakdown for it.** But the DTI filing did, and it was reported consistently:

| Model | Model years | Units |
|---|---|---|
| **Civic 4D** | **2022–2024** | **2,463** |
| CR-V | 2024 | 2,762 |
| **Civic Type R** | **2023–2025** | **280** |
| | **Total** | **5,505** |

Those three figures sum to exactly 5,505, and two independent outlets report the identical split — which is why we are treating this as the campaign's real composition rather than as speculation.

**What that means in practice.** Between the two campaigns, **the Civic is the model with the largest recall exposure of any Honda in this market**, and the 2022 model year appears in both. We are not going to guess at how the two overlap — HCPI published them as separate announcements, and the March 2024 one names only "select 2022 Year Model" cars while the January 2025 one spans 2022–2024. **If you own a 2022 Civic, do not try to reason out which campaign applies to you.** Put your **17-digit chassis number or VIN** into the search tool at `hondaphil.com/recall`. It is free, it takes a minute, and it is definitive for your specific car.

### The correction this creates

Our [HR-V guide](/blog/honda-hr-v-pms-guide) and [CR-V guide](/blog/honda-cr-v-pms-guide) both said that HCPI published no model list for the 5,505-unit steering recall — which was accurate about HCPI's own pages, and is why both posts told readers to check their VIN rather than speculate. The DTI-sourced breakdown above answers it: **the campaign is Civic, CR-V and Civic Type R only.** The **HR-V is not in it.** We would rather say that plainly here than leave the better information buried.

**And on the fuel pump:** the Civic is **not** in the current high-pressure fuel pump recall — that one covers **1,351 units of the 2023–2024 CR-V RS e:HEV.** The Civic *was* named in the older **10,136-unit fuel pump campaign of 2018–2019 model years**, which is FC-era territory and is covered in the [FC](/blog/honda-civic-fc-pms-guide) and [Jazz](/blog/honda-jazz-pms-guide) guides.

## Known Issues, Honestly

**Oil dilution — and the engine code matters.** The FE's turbo is the **L15CA**, an evolution of the FC's **L15B7**. The L15B7's oil-dilution issue is thoroughly documented: fuel passes the piston rings during cold running, dilutes the oil, and produces a fuel smell at the dipstick with a **rising** oil level. Overseas owner reports indicate the behaviour did not vanish with the L15CA.

Two caveats decide how much this should weigh on you, and they are the same ones we set out on the [FC](/blog/honda-civic-fc-pms-guide):

1. **It is a cold-climate mechanism.** It depends on the engine taking a long time to reach operating temperature so unburnt fuel does not evaporate off and get routed back through the crankcase ventilation. Philippine ambient temperatures work against it, and PH sources report it as rare here.
2. **There is no HCPI recall or technical bulletin for it**, and the material we could find on the L15CA specifically is **overseas owner-forum reporting**, not manufacturer documentation or a local defect pattern.

That is not a reason to be anxious. It is a reason to do the boring thing: **check your oil level periodically**, especially if your driving is mostly short city hops, and do not stretch the interval.

**Low ground clearance.** 134 mm is a design decision, not a fault, but it is the FE's most reliable source of ownership cost — undercarriage scrapes on humps, ramps and flooded roads. If your daily route has a steep village entrance, drive one over it before you buy.

**And what we did not find.** We found **no documented widespread mechanical failure pattern for the Philippine FE**, and **no PH recall beyond the two steering campaigns.** We are reporting that as "we did not find one," not as "there is none." The car has been here since late 2021, which is enough time for a pattern to surface but not enough to be confident about long-term wear.

## Safety: Read the Denominators

**5 stars at ASEAN NCAP**, with an overall score of **83.47 out of 100**, tested in **October 2021** under the 2021–2025 assessment protocol. The published pillar scores:

| Pillar | Score |
|---|---|
| Adult Occupant Protection | **29.28 / 32** |
| Child Occupant Protection | **46.72 / 51** |
| Safety Assist | **19.07 / 21** |
| Motorcyclist Safety | **8.32 / 16** |

Within AOP, the car took **full marks (8.00/8.00) for side impact**, **14.54/16** for frontal impact, and **6.74/8** for head-protection technology.

**Two things to be careful about here**, because comparing safety scores is where car research goes wrong most often.

**First, the denominators do not match our other Honda posts.** We published the [HR-V](/blog/honda-hr-v-pms-guide) at 35.00/**40** for AOP and the [CR-V](/blog/honda-cr-v-pms-guide) at 39.31/**40**. The Civic's AOP is published as 29.28/**32**, with COP out of 51 and Safety Assist out of 21. Those are different reporting bases from the same programme. **Only the star rating and the /100 overall score are comparable** — the raw pillar numbers are not, and anyone putting them in one table is misleading you.

**Second, the tested car was a Thailand-market EL+**, and the rating was issued for units sold in Thailand, Singapore and Indonesia, equipped with six airbags and Honda SENSING as standard. Honda SENSING is standard across the PH range too, which is the meaningful part — but **the crash-tested unit was not a Philippine-spec car.**

## The Type R (FL5): Out of Scope, and On Its Way Out

The **Civic Type R FL5** is an 11th-generation Civic, so it belongs in this guide's family tree — but not in its cost tables. **K20C, 325 hp, 420 Nm, six-speed manual, front-wheel drive**, and a maintenance, tyre and parts profile that has nothing to do with a CVT commuter sedan. It is a JDM-vertical subject, exactly as we said when we drew the same line around the [FK8 on the FC guide](/blog/honda-civic-fc-pms-guide).

Three facts about it that do belong here:

- **It launched on 19 January 2023 at ₱3.88 million**, with a first batch of just **40 units** — 39 sold to customers and one kept as a demonstrator — against **more than 100 orders placed before launch.** Further batches followed.
- **HCPI has announced the current Type R's PH run is concluding**, with only the last remaining **2025 Year Model** units available. The announcement frames it as the end of an era rather than a permanent farewell to the nameplate.
- **It is in the steering recall: 280 units, 2023–2025 model years.** If you own one, run the VIN like everyone else.

If you want the enthusiast Civic story properly — the SiR lineage, the B-series, what these cars become — that is our [Civic SiR EK4 guide](/blog/honda-civic-sir-guide), and the FL5 is the obvious next chapter.

## Casa vs. Talyer

Under warranty — **3 years or 100,000 km** — keep it at the casa and keep the receipts. Two FE-specific points sharpen the usual advice.

**The five-year warranty is not yours.** **Honda Secure Plus (5 years / 150,000 km) covers the Prelude and the 2026 CR-V only.** Not the Civic. This is the third time we have had to flag this exact misreading, after the [BR-V](/blog/honda-br-v-pms-guide) and [HR-V](/blog/honda-hr-v-pms-guide). Yours is three years.

**On the hybrid battery, we have no Civic-specific figure.** HCPI publishes **8 years / 160,000 km** for the 2026 CR-V's pack, and an **8 years / 200,000 km** figure circulates for earlier e:HEV cars. **We found nothing published specifically for the Civic RS e:HEV.** Do not assume either number applies — **get the exact terms written into your paperwork**, and understand that a battery warranty is worthless without a documented service history.

Out of warranty, the split is the usual one with two exceptions:

- **The CVT on the turbo variants** is where casa work pays for itself, entirely on the HCF-2 question.
- **The hybrid system on the RS e:HEV** is casa work, full stop — high-voltage components, Honda-specific diagnostics, and a battery warranty that depends on documented history.

Oil, brakes, filters, tyres and suspension a competent independent handles fine. Our [look at when the casa premium is worth paying](/blog/casa-vs-talyer-philippines) works through the trade-off in general.

Network context: Honda runs roughly **38 dealerships plus three service centres** nationwide — thinner than Toyota's or Mitsubishi's. Check what is near you, and weight it heavily if you are buying the hybrid.

## How the FE Compares

- **Versus the [FC it replaced](/blog/honda-civic-fc-pms-guide):** the FE is better equipped, has Honda SENSING standard across the range, a stronger turbo, and a hybrid option. What it lost is the **cheap entry point** — the FC's 1.8 S and the FE's own discontinued S Turbo were both considerably less money than today's ₱1,600,000 floor. If budget is the binding constraint, a late FC is the value play.
- **Versus the [Honda City](/blog/honda-city-pms-guide):** the same brand advantages — 10,000 km interval, timing chain, HCF-2 discipline — for dramatically less money. The Civic's case is size, refinement, the turbo and the hybrid. The City's case is that it does the same job for most people.
- **Versus the Toyota Corolla Altis Hybrid:** the direct fight, and Toyota's hybrid has far more years on Philippine roads plus a much denser dealer network. The Civic answers with more power (184 PS against the Altis hybrid's output) and the better-resolved chassis. If you want a Toyota hybrid crossover instead, the [Corolla Cross](/blog/toyota-corolla-cross-pms-guide) is the cross-shop.
- **Versus its own [CR-V](/blog/honda-cr-v-pms-guide) and [HR-V](/blog/honda-hr-v-pms-guide) siblings:** same interval, same fluid rules, same e:HEV logic. The Civic is the one you buy if you actually want a low, quick sedan rather than a crossover — and at 134 mm of clearance, that preference has to be genuine.

## Who This Car Is Actually For

**Buy the V Turbo** if you want the FE's engine and Honda SENSING at the lowest entry point and can live without the RS's cosmetic and equipment upgrades. It is the same 178 PS drivetrain as the RS.

**Buy the RS Turbo** if the styling, Sport mode and interior matter to you — it is the enthusiast-adjacent choice among the CVT cars, and the one most likely to hold attention on resale.

**Buy the RS e:HEV** if you do a lot of city driving and want the most powerful non-Type-R Civic that also happens to be the most efficient. The absence of a CVT belt is a real long-term ownership argument that no salesperson will make for you. Price the premium over the RS Turbo against your actual annual fuel spend first.

**Think harder if** your route punishes a 134 mm car, if you need a cheap entry into a Civic (look at a late FC), or if you live far from a Honda dealer and are considering the hybrid.

## The Bottom Line

The Civic FE is an unusually low-maintenance turbocharged car: **10,000 km or six months, timing chain, no belt job, and no shortened interval for the boost.** It carries **5 stars and 83.47 points at ASEAN NCAP**, Honda SENSING is standard on every variant, and the RS e:HEV is both the most powerful and the most efficient Civic you can buy here short of a Type R.

The things worth knowing before you sign are these. **Every FE is turbo** — the cheap NA Civic no longer exists, and the S Turbo that partly filled that gap has been discontinued. **The turbo variants need Honda HCF-2 and nothing else**, and no published kilometre interval exists for changing it, so get one written down. **The "E-CVT" is not a CVT.** **The warranty is three years, not five** — Secure Plus is not for this car. **That 30 km/L figure came off a 155-kilometre expressway run**, so treat it as a ceiling. And **two steering recalls name this car**, one of them accounting for 2,463 units of 2022–2024 Civics.

None of that makes the FE a bad buy. Recalls are free work, and a manufacturer that files a breakdown with the DTI is behaving correctly. What it makes the FE is a car you should **run the VIN on first** — before the test drive, before the trade-in valuation, before anything. Then get the fluid interval in the booklet and pick the variant on how you actually drive.
