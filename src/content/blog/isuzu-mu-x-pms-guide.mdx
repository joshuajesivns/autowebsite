The **Isuzu mu-X** is what happens when you take the [D-Max pickup](/blog/isuzu-d-max-pms-guide) and ask it to carry a family instead of cargo. Same 4JJ3 engine, same ladder frame, same 10,000 km service rhythm — but with seven seats, a coil-sprung rear end, and a school run to survive.

That shared DNA is the whole point. It means the mu-X inherits the D-Max's genuinely good ownership economics: **a 10,000 km PMS interval** (half the casa visits of a Fortuner), **two free services** to start, and **timing chains on both engines** so there's no five-figure belt job waiting in the future. It also inherits the D-Max's weak spot — **a short warranty at 3 years/100,000 km**, which the Ford Everest beats outright.

So magkano ba talaga to imaintain, at para kanino ito? Below: the full interval chart, honest cost estimates, the chain answer, what real owners report, a variant-level safety catch worth knowing before you sign, and the model-year split that matters if you're buying used. For the wider context on what each service stage covers, start with our [explainer on PMS stages and what each one costs](/blog/car-pms-philippines-guide).

## Key Takeaways

- **PMS schedule:** every **10,000 km or 6 months**, whichever comes first — and the **first two services (1,500 km and 5,000 km) are free.**
- **Light PMS** runs roughly **₱4,500–7,000**; heavy/major stops land around **₱10,000–15,000**. *(Estimates — Isuzu PH publishes no price list. Verify with your dealer, August 2026.)*
- **Timing belt or chain?** **Chain on both engines** — the 1.9L RZ4E-TC and the 3.0L 4JJ3-TCX. No scheduled belt replacement, ever.
- **Warranty is the catch: 3 years / 100,000 km** — matched by the Montero Sport, but beaten by the Fortuner's extension (5 years / 140,000 km) and comfortably by the Everest's 5 years / 150,000 km. Plan around it if you drive long distances.
- **Owner sentiment is genuinely strong:** **4.27/5 across 41 Zigwheels PH reviews** (40 happy, 1 not), with ride comfort the most repeated praise.
- **Airbags vary by variant** — and the sourcing is muddy enough that you should confirm on your variant's brochure, not the model page.
- **A real PH recall exists:** 1,579 mu-X units built 2020–Oct 2022, steering-shaft bolt torque, free repair.

## Which Model Years Does This Guide Cover? (2021–2026)

This guide covers the **second-generation mu-X**, and there's a detail here worth knowing: **the Philippines got it first.** Isuzu Philippines launched the all-new mu-X on **September 22, 2021**, making the PH the **first left-hand-drive market in the world** to receive it — unusual for a market our size, and a reflection of how well Isuzu sells here.

There are two phases within that generation:

| Phase | Period | What defines it |
|---|---|---|
| **Original 2nd-gen** | Sept 2021 – early 2025 | Launch styling, 5 variants at introduction, smaller infotainment screen |
| **Refreshed 2nd-gen** | **April 4, 2025** onward | Aggressive new fascia, Dynamic Blade headlights, connected tail lights, electroluminescent instrument panel, 10.1-inch touchscreen with wireless Apple CarPlay/Android Auto |

The refresh that launched on **April 4, 2025** is what's sold today as the 2026 model — AutoIndustriya described it as the second generation's **first proper update**. Mechanically, **nothing changed where it matters for maintenance**: same two engines, same 6-speed automatic, same 10,000 km interval. So every schedule and cost figure in this guide applies across the whole 2021–2026 run. What changes between the phases is screens and styling, not service.

Two smaller updates sit inside the earlier phase too — a quiet styling tweak with new wheels, and a **2024 addition of a digital video recorder (DVR)**. Neither affects servicing.

| 2nd | 5,000 km | Initial check | **Free** |

| 5th | 30,000 km | Light PMS | Paid |

| — | every 10,000 km after | alternating light / heavy | — |

The practical meaning: **two casa visits a year at average PH mileage.** A **Toyota Fortuner** owner, on Toyota's 5,000 km cycle, makes four. Over five years that's roughly **ten visits versus twenty** — and that gap, not the per-visit price, is where the mu-X actually saves you money. ## Light PMS vs. Heavy PMS on the mu-X

A **light PMS** — most of your visits — is engine oil and filter, a fuel/water separator drain, tire rotation, brake inspection, and a multi-point check of hoses, belts, lights and undercarriage. On a diesel this size, the oil change alone is a meaningful share of the bill.

A **heavy (major) PMS** at **40,000 km and 80,000 km** adds the expensive consumables: fuel filter, air filter, brake fluid, coolant, transmission fluid, differential oil, and — on the 4x4 — **transfer case fluid**. That last item is why a 4x4 LS-E heavy PMS costs more than a 4x2's, and it's the one shops most often skip if you don't ask.

The item to watch on any ladder-frame diesel SUV in PH conditions isn't in the PMS menu at all: **the undercarriage.** Bushings, ball joints, tie rods, and shock absorbers wear on our roads faster than the schedule anticipates, and a mu-X's soft, comfort-tuned suspension hides the early symptoms better than a stiffer truck would. Have the pang-ilalim inspected at every heavy stop.

## Isuzu mu-X: Timing Belt or Chain?

**Chain — on both engines, no exceptions.** The 3.0L 4JJ3-TCX and the 1.9L RZ4E-TC both use a timing chain designed to last the life of the engine, with no scheduled replacement interval.

This is a real, quantifiable advantage over one specific rival. The **Ford Everest** uses a **"wet" timing belt** (belt-in-oil) with a scheduled replacement — a design that has drawn genuine reliability questions, including from PH reviewers. If you're cross-shopping those two, see our [Ford Everest maintenance and cost breakdown](/blog/ford-everest-pms-guide) for the full picture on that.

The caveat worth stating plainly: **"lifetime chain" means "lifetime if you change the oil on time."** Timing chains don't snap the way neglected belts do — they stretch, and the plastic guides they run against wear. Both failure modes are driven by dirty or overdue oil. A rattling chain on cold start is the warning sign, and by then it's an expensive repair. The chain isn't maintenance-free; it's maintenance-by-proxy, and the proxy is your oil discipline.

## The Warranty — 3 Years / 100,000 km

This is the mu-X's genuine weak spot, and it deserves more attention than it usually gets:

| Vehicle | Warranty |
|---|---|
| **Isuzu mu-X** | **3 years / 100,000 km** |
| Toyota Fortuner | 3 years / 100,000 km, extendable to 5 years / 140,000 km |
| Mitsubishi Montero Sport | 3 years / 100,000 km — the same as the mu-X |
| Ford Everest | **5 years / 150,000 km** — the segment's most generous |

Isuzu Philippines states **36 months or 100,000 kilometres, whichever comes first** on its own site. Read that "whichever comes first" carefully, because it's where the cost lands: at **30,000 km a year** — an ordinary figure for a provincial family SUV or anything doing regular Manila–province runs — **you hit 100,000 km at around 40 months, and the warranty has already expired months before the three-year mark.** The years never get used.

That's the trade the mu-X asks you to make: **cheaper routine servicing, less coverage when something big goes wrong.** For a low-mileage city SUV it barely matters. For a high-mileage provincial hauler — the exact buyer the mu-X courts — it matters a lot, and it's worth pricing an extended warranty into the deal rather than discovering the gap at 90,000 km.

## The Airbag Question — Check Your Variant, Not the Model Page

Here's a discrepancy we could not resolve, so we'll show you both sides rather than pick one.

When the second-generation mu-X launched in 2021, PH coverage was explicit: **the top-spec LS-E got seven airbags, and everything below it got two** — driver and front passenger only. Isuzu's **current model page appears to list seven airbags across all four variants**, which would mean a meaningful safety upgrade somewhere in the intervening years.

We're not treating that as settled, for a specific reason: **the same table on that page also states the wrong engine displacement for the base RZ4E variant** — listing it as 3.0L when the RZ4E-TC is a 1.9L. A table that propagates the top variant's numbers into other rows can't be trusted at the variant level, which is exactly the level you need.

**So: get the brochure for the specific variant you're buying, and count the airbags on that document.** This is the same trap the D-Max presents, and it's not unique to Isuzu — a manufacturer model page advertises the best-equipped trim, and the unit in the showroom may not be it.

On the safety record itself, the news is good and verified: the mu-X earned a **five-star ASEAN NCAP rating with 86.81 out of 100**, including **15.85 of 18** for safety assist. Note the date, though — that assessment is from the **2021 launch car**, under the protocol in force then.

## Known Issues — What's Actually Documented

Owner sentiment is strong: **4.27 out of 5 across 41 Zigwheels PH reviews** — 14 rated it excellent, 26 very good, and exactly one owner was unhappy. That's a small sample, and we'll say so, but it's real PH owner data rather than overseas forum noise. One owner reported **200 km weekly Manila–province runs on a six-year-old unit with no major issues.**

What's actually documented, honestly sorted:

- **The 2023 steering-shaft recall (real, dated, PH-specific).** May 8, 2023: **1,579 mu-X units** built between 2020 and October 2022, lower second steering shaft bolt tightened below specified torque, free inspection and repair. If you're buying used from that window, confirm the campaign was done.
- **Infotainment and electronics niggles.** AutoIndustriya's test of the 2026 LS-E 4x4 reported **wireless Android Auto needing repeated pairing attempts** and the **keyless door unlock malfunctioning**. Annoyances, not failures — but they're on the newest, most expensive variant.
- **An over-sensitive forward-collision warning.** Same review. Common across the segment; more irritating in heavy traffic.
- **Hydraulic, not electric, power steering.** A deliberate engineering choice rather than a fault — it suits towing and off-road feel, but it's heavier at parking speeds and dates the car against rivals.
- **Soft suspension is a trade, not a defect.** The most-praised trait (that comfortable ride) is also the one criticism owners raise: on uneven, rough surfaces it can feel floaty. This is tuning, working as intended.
- **Gloss black trim scratches easily.** Cosmetic, universal to the trend.

**What we're deliberately not claiming:** there is **no verified PH-specific mechanical defect pattern** on the second-generation mu-X beyond the recall above. The 4JJ3 has a strong durability reputation — 300,000 km-plus with proper maintenance is well attested — and inventing a problems list to fill a section would be dishonest. Overseas EGR and DPF complaints exist on this engine family, but they come from Euro 5/6 markets whose emissions hardware **the PH-spec Euro 4 truck may not even have.**

One caution about researching this yourself: **searches for "Isuzu mu-X problems" now surface AI-generated content farms** that assemble plausible-sounding fault lists with no sourcing. We found several while writing this. If a page can't name a date, a market, or a unit count, treat it as fiction.

## Casa vs. Talyer & Parts

While the warranty is live — remember, that may be **sooner than three years** if you drive a lot — stay at the casa and keep every receipt. Isuzu's dealer network is smaller than Toyota's but well distributed, and the brand's commercial-vehicle roots mean service departments are used to working trucks rather than only family cars.

After warranty, the mu-X is a strong talyer candidate. The 4JJ3 is a widely understood engine with excellent parts availability, and independent diesel specialists in PH know it well. The exceptions that should stay at the casa: **ECU work, anything under an active recall campaign** (free, but casa-only), and diagnostic work on the driver-assist systems.

For the full decision framework — including what the warranty terms actually permit and where the price gap is widest — see our [breakdown of casa versus talyer servicing](/blog/casa-vs-talyer-philippines).

## Buying Used: What to Check

- **Run the VIN against the 2023 steering-shaft campaign** if the unit was built 2020–October 2022. Free to fix, and a seller who doesn't know is telling you something about how they've maintained it.
- **Count the airbags on the actual unit**, per the section above. Don't rely on the listing.
- **Check the service book against the 10,000 km interval.** Because the interval is long, a single skipped service is a much bigger deal here than on a 5,000 km car — one missed stop can mean 20,000 km on one oil fill.
- **Listen for chain rattle on a genuinely cold start.** This is the single most informative 10 seconds of a used-diesel inspection.
- **Inspect the undercarriage.** Soft suspension masks worn bushings; get it on a lift.
- **Confirm which phase you're looking at** — pre- or post-April 2025 refresh. It doesn't change the mechanicals, but it changes the price it should command.

## The Bottom Line

The mu-X makes a coherent, unglamorous argument: **the cheapest midsize diesel SUV to service in its class, with a drivetrain that routinely runs past 300,000 km, wrapped in the most comfortable ride in the segment.** Two casa visits a year instead of four, two of them free at the start, and no timing belt to budget for.

The price of that is the warranty. **Three years or 100,000 km — whichever comes first** — matches the Montero Sport but trails the Fortuner's extension and the Everest's 5 years/150,000 km, and for the high-mileage provincial buyer this SUV is built for, the kilometre limit will arrive well before the calendar one. Go in knowing that, and it's an easy car to recommend.
